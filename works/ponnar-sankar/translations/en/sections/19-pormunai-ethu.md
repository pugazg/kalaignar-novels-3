16

Where Is the Battlefront?

Ponnar raised his voice and addressed the Aarichampatti warriors who were following behind them.

“Warriors! Stay exactly where you are and use your shields to stop the arrows! We must begin our counterattack only after a large share of the arrows in the enemy's possession has been exhausted. Until then, use our strength only to block and endure their attack.”

This idea of Ponnar's was new to the Aarichampatti warriors. At first they did not grasp the meaning of the strategy proposed by Ponnar and Sankar, trained in warfare: instead of striking back the instant the enemy attacked, they would first tire the enemy until its strength had been reduced by half and only then attack. Yet, treating it as the command of their commanders, the Aarichampatti warriors obeyed and acted accordingly.

From inside Raachchaandaar Malai Fort, Thirumalai, commander of Thalaiyur Kaali, was issuing orders to his warriors. Beside Thirumalai, Maanthiyappan held a cup of liquor, laughed boisterously and gulped drink after drink.

In a richly decorated room near the place where the two men sat giving orders to the warriors, Vaiyam Perumaan had been bound with heavy—

<!-- source boundary: scan 155 → scan 156 -->

—chains and tied to a great pillar. Two rough warriors stood on either side of the pillar, heavy swords in their hands and their eyes wide open, preventing anyone from approaching him.

Two teakwood beds with legs made of ivory stood in that room. On one bed Silambayi sat as though grief itself had taken form. Facing her, Muthayi sat clasping her knees, tears brimming in her eyes. On the other bed, Pavalayi sat with her head bowed onto her knees, tears running down her cheeks. The uproar of battle outside the fort kept reaching their ears. They were certain that it must be Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu who had come with an army.

Even in a world containing people like Chellaatha Gounder and Maanthiyappan, who had resolved to live by betrayal, rebellion and brutality so long as they could somehow enlarge their own selfish interests, there were also many like Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu who had bound themselves to the resolve that, if they were to live in the illustrious historical record of the Kongu Vellalar clan, they must live with valour and honour. It was upon that firm belief that the prisoners concluded that the man who had brought an army and surrounded the fort must indeed be Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu—yes, it could only be him.

Yet they could not rejoice merely because of that. Nor could they take comfort. The number of troops massed at Raachchaandaar Malai under Thirumalai's command was very great. They had firmly concluded that the Aarichampatti force, not even half as numerous, could not defeat Commander Thirumalai, capture the fort and rescue them.

Several Raachchaandaar Malai warriors came running urgently to where Maanthiyappan and Thirumalai were seated. “What is it? Why are you running like this?” Thirumalai asked.

<!-- source boundary: scan 156 → scan 157 -->

“The Aarichampatti warriors are not advancing at all! They are standing exactly where they stopped, on their horses! All they can do is block with their shields the arrows that fly from us like a swarm!” the warriors said. Drunk and wild, Maanthiyappan shouted, “Sabash!” and burst into rattling laughter. Then he cried out in a terrifying voice like a wolf.

“The fellows must be trembling! They must be struggling even to move a single step forward! Do they think Commander Thirumalai is some mere weakling? I am coming now. I shall look through the opening in the fort battlement and enjoy that pitiful sight.” Saying this, Maanthiyappan patted Thirumalai on the shoulder, called two of the warriors who had come there, and went toward the front of the battlement.

Maanthiyappan looked through that opening, which was slightly smaller than a window but arranged so that one could clearly see the wide space outside from within, and was struck with some surprise and bewilderment.

What startled him was the sight of Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu at the front of the Aarichampatti formation; beside him were the two young men who had leapt into the river and saved Muthayi and Pavalayi, and with them another warrior. Their daring deed in defiance of the whirlwind, their deadly struggle with the mountain snake and the crocodile—when all this came back to his mind, doubt seized him: although Thirumalai's army was large, could it withstand men of such courage?

Maanthiyappan turned his eyes away from the Aarichampatti army and stood upright, lost in thought. His attendant held out another cup filled with liquor. Within moments of sending it into his stomach, the sudden fear and doubt that had arisen in him flew away. The tingling force of the liquor—

<!-- source boundary: scan 157 → scan 158 -->

—poured new vigour and extraordinary boldness into his heart.

He returned from there to Thirumalai. Thirumalai, meanwhile, kept ordering his warriors to rain arrows without interruption from the fort and watched grandly as the order was carried out.

Maanthiyappan told Commander Thirumalai that those young men had come leading the Aarichampatti army. Because he did not know their names, he described what had happened in the Kaveri during the festival of releasing the *karagam*, and warned Thirumalai not to underestimate the young men's abilities. But the courage Thirumalai drew from the intoxication of authority was even greater than the courage Maanthiyappan had drawn from the intoxication of liquor.

“What does it matter who has come? What can mouse-pups do to this Himalaya? They will be crushed so completely no one will even know where they died!” Thirumalai roared. “Yes! Let it be so!” he ordered his warriors again.

Without a moment's hesitation, arrows continued to pour from inside the fort toward the Aarichampatti force.

The Aarichampatti warriors kept blocking the arrows with their shields, but one fell from his horse, then another, then ten, then a hundred. As the warriors fell, some horses began to run. Others were struck by arrows, collapsed there and writhed.

As these reports arrived one after another, Maanthiyappan, dancing in an unbearable frenzy of delight, strode proudly toward the room where the Aarichampatti family was confined.

The door to the room was locked, and it had been bolted from inside as well. Through the glass window beside the door, Maanthiyappan looked at those within and—

<!-- source boundary: scan 158 → scan 159 -->

—burst into a mocking laugh. They poured their hatred through their eyes like fire. Angered by it, Maanthiyappan swung the hilt of his hand-sword against the glass of the window and smashed it.

Now those inside the room could hear him clearly.

“O jewels among maidens, created by Maayan solely so that you may marry me! Not only has your rough father come to rescue you; those rough young men who fought the crocodile and the mountain snake and saved you have come too.”

The moment these words reached their ears, not only Muthayi and Pavalayi but Silambayi and Vaiyam Perumaan too felt a measure of comfort—a measure of hope.

Noticing the change in their faces, Maanthiyappan laughed dismissively, kissed his cup of liquor, threw it down, and said, “Oho! Do you think you will be safe because those fellows have come? That will not happen! The army here is not a Kaveri crocodile or a mountain snake. More than half your Aarichampatti army has already been destroyed in the attack we are conducting from the fort! In a little while Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu and those little fellows who came to help him will be captured too. After that comes my wedding! Muthayi on one side, Pavalayi on the other—and I shall stand between them like Lord Kanna Paramatma between Bama and Rukmani!”

No one inside the room answered him. Some hope had arisen within them, and they felt there was no point wasting words on him like water poured upon barren ground, so they remained silent.

Frenzied delight and the peak strength of drunkenness competed with each other in driving Maanthiyappan. Leaving the window of that room, he returned to the place where Commander Thirumalai was.

<!-- source boundary: scan 159 → scan 160 -->

Thirumalai looked greatly elated. The reason was that the Aarichampatti warriors lay collapsed under the attack of the Raachchaandaar Malai force, as though they had come seeking death on the battlefield by throwing away their own lives. One of the last warriors to arrive from the battlement area reported:

“Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu is sitting absolutely motionless on his horse. The three young men who came to support him and lead the army are sitting the same way on their horses. They are only blocking the arrows we shoot with their shields, handling them with remarkable ease. Apart from that, the Aarichampatti army has been completely destroyed!”

Thirumalai patted that warrior approvingly and burst into loud laughter. Then he issued a new order to his troops.

“Enough! Stop raining arrows! Do not kill Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu. Let him and one or two of those who came with him survive. Should not the girls' father see their wedding? Should he not offer his blessings?”

At their commander's order, the rain of arrows from Raachchaandaar Malai Fort stopped.

Maanthiyappan expressed a wish of his own to Commander Thirumalai.

“Thirumalai! The Aarichampatti man is standing outside our fort. Bring Muthayi and Pavalayi to the fort battlement right now. Before his very eyes I shall place garlands around the necks of both women. That is how Aarichampatti must be humiliated!”

Thirumalai did not agree to Maanthiyappan's wish.

“Your marriage to the Aarichampatti princesses is going to take place anyway. Before that—

<!-- source boundary: scan 160 → scan 161 -->

—why this haste? It is not only haste; it would not be proper conduct either. Thalaiyur Kaali wants everything to happen according to due form. If he hears of this, he will rage at me. So I will not agree.”

Hearing this reply from Thirumalai, Maanthiyappan stood there looking foolish.

At that moment Commander Thirumalai was informed that a Thalaiyur warrior was approaching Raachchaandaar Malai Fort on horseback. It was then reported that the arriving warrior had shown a ring bearing the Thalaiyur seal at the fort entrance and was coming inside. A few moments later, the Thalaiyur warrior placed a palm-leaf scroll in Commander Thirumalai's hand, bowed respectfully and stood aside.

The commander finished reading the palm leaf in agitation. Maanthiyappan stared at his face as though asking, “What urgent message is it?”

“Muthayi, Pavalayi and the others are to be brought from Raachchaandaar Malai to Thalaiyur at once. The wedding is to take place in Thalaiyur itself. The King of Thalaiyur apparently considers this the safer arrangement. King Kaali has ordered us to bring the Aarichampatti family to Thalaiyur immediately.”

After briefly summarising what the letter said, Thirumalai prepared to carry out the order of the King of Thalaiyur.

A chariot was brought and stopped at the fort gate. Several warriors on horseback surrounded it as guards.

Thirumalai went to the room where the Aarichampatti family was confined. The outer lock on the door was opened. The bolt on the inside could not be opened, and those inside refused to open it. Sensing that a new danger was coming, they trembled. Realising that they had not been rescued—

<!-- source boundary: scan 161 → scan 162 -->

—their thoughts reeled. The warriors put a hand through the window that had already been broken and opened the inner bolt of the door.

Thirumalai entered. He told them the news respectfully. Maanthiyappan was cavorting without restraint.

“We are leaving for Thalaiyur! The Aarichampatti army has been destroyed! Those who came to rescue you are standing there stunned! It is their good fortune that they too have not been arrested. King Kaali of Thalaiyur greatly desires that, when the auspicious wedding takes place in Thalaiyur, your father should come and offer his blessings. Please set out. Do not make trouble!”

Hearing these words from Thirumalai, they merely stood like statues of sorrow; they made no trouble.

Silambayi, Muthayi and Pavalayi were brought to the chariot waiting at the fort entrance. After seating them in the chariot, Vaiyam Perumaan too was put into it, still bound in chains.

Commander Thirumalai and Maanthiyappan mounted two imposing horses.

From some distance outside the fort, Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu, Ponnar, Sankar and Veeramalai watched this scene without blinking from their horses. The chariot sped away from the fort entrance.

After passing the entrance and then the fort's second encircling wall, as the chariot entered through the next gateway, a tremendous cry arose: “Vel! Vel! Veera Vel! Vetri Vel!” Yes—it was the cry raised by Ponnar, Sankar and Veeramalai.

Before that cry had died away, all but one of the Aarichampatti warriors who had been lying fallen outside the fort—

<!-- source boundary: scan 162 → scan 163 -->

—sprang upright. In the very act of rising, they attacked the Raachchaandaar Malai warriors who had come surrounding the chariot.

Ponnar and Sankar, drawn swords in hand, plunged like a storm into that mass of warriors. Commander Thirumalai engaged Sankar. Ponnar fought the Raachchaandaar Malai warriors alongside the Aarichampatti men. In the midst of it, Veeramalai leapt into the chariot and cut apart the chains binding Vaiyam Perumaan. Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu writhed in alarm, fearing that Commander Thirumalai's sword might split Sankar's head; at that moment Sankar drove his own sword into that mountain of flesh's chest and brought him down.

Seeing their commander dead, the Raachchaandaar Malai warriors scattered and fled. Before more warriors could come from inside the fort, Vaiyam Perumaan took the reins of the chariot and drove it, with Ponnar, Sankar, Veeramalai, Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu and the Aarichampatti warriors following behind.

The chariot flew faster than the wind.

In the confusion, Maanthiyappan fled headlong on the horse he had mounted and somehow escaped.
