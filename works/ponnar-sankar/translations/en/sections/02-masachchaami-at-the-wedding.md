Masachchaami at the Wedding

The village known both as Aarichampatti and Maniyangurichi wore the look of a festival. Fields already flourishing from the swelling Kaveri; crops gently nodding in those fields; groves filled with luxuriant coconut palms rising high and bearing bunch upon bunch; fertile land in no way lacking in water—at Maniyangurichi, also called Aarichampatti, in that region, the men of the village's major landholder Malaikkozhundha Gounder worked with great enthusiasm to make the third day of the three-day festivities surpass the first two. Throughout the village, the streets were hung with festoons of coconut fronds and mango leaves. At street corners, tall, strong bamboo poles had been planted and bunch-laden banana plants tied to them.

Not only inside the village but even along the road leading to it, banana plants embraced the bamboo poles and, heedless of having been cut down, played with the wind through their broad, long leaves. Cages of many shapes made from papers of many colours had been hung beautifully at several points in the middle of the streets. Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu, son of the major landholder Malaikkozhundha Gounder, and his mother Perumayi Ammal, Malaikkozhundha Gounder's wife, saw the whole village looking like an ornament and their hearts filled with

<!-- source boundary: scan 18 → scan 19 -->

delight. Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu moved briskly through every part of the village, energetically urging on the workers engaged in the arrangements for the third day's festivities.

At the entrance of Malaikkozhundha Gounder's house, a small mansion built high enough to make one lift one's head and look up, with lime mortar, a huge covered pavilion had been erected. More than fifty men spun about like tops in the work of decorating it. Malaikkozhundha Gounder watched the bustling work while reclining in an easy chair at the front of the house. Sitting at his feet, Perumayi Ammal folded betel leaves and gave them to her husband, chewed betel herself, and filled her face with happiness as she thought of the event to take place the next day.

Near that house, along the bank of the pond, stood a long pavilion; the sound of drums could be heard there. Thick plaits of palm leaves were woven closely and hung along the edges of the pavilion roof. At every pavilion post were clusters of date-palm fruit and bunches of mango leaves. Along the length of the pavilion, a huge crowd stood one behind another in a line. There could not have been fewer than a thousand people. All were poor men and women dressed in dirty, torn or worn-out clothes.

There was an eagerness in their eyes; every gaze was fixed on the slightly raised platform in the middle of the pavilion. The reason was that large baskets on that platform held the food grains that were to be given to them.

Rice, varagu, kambu and cholam—many kinds of food grain were heaped on that platform, and the thought that they were going to be scooped up and

<!-- source boundary: scan 19 → scan 20 -->

given to them free—would that not bring unbearable joy to the destitute?

A three-day festival. What was taking place now was the second day's celebration. Tomorrow's third-day celebration would be the crowning event of the entire festival. The village decorations and the arrangements for distributing food grain were all connected with it. Then what festival was it?

There stood Chozhan Thotti in a public place in the village, in the shade of the Amman temple, beating the drum. Listen carefully to what announcement he was making and every detail would become clear.

“This is to inform all the people of the village that, in view of the wedding to be held tomorrow morning between Thamarai Naachchiyar, daughter of the major landholder Malaikkozhundha Gounder of Aarichampatti, also called Maniyangurichi, and Mathiyappa Gounder, son of Chellaatha Gounder, the ruler in charge of frontier protection in Nelli Valanaadu, free food grain is being distributed today to the poor by the bride Thamarai Naachchiyar with her own hands. The major landholder requests everyone to come, receive it and bless the bride and groom.”

Between the “dam, dam” beats of the drum, Chozhan Thotti repeated this three times and then set off for the next village. The poor who had gathered to hear the announcement surged toward the pavilion where the food grain was being distributed.

Alongside Chozhan Thotti, who had been born in the Adi Dravidar community and was past fifty, a boy of about six or seven walked quickly. Seeing them going along with the drum, a street dog chased them barking. The boy became fiercely angry. He picked up a stone from the street and raised his arm to throw it at the dog. “Hey, hey... Veeramalai! Poor thing, don't hit the dog. It is a creature without speech—it must have been frightened and is

<!-- source boundary: scan 20 → scan 21 -->

barking. Come with me!” Saying this, Chozhan Thotti snatched the stone from the boy's hand, threw it away and started walking. “Appa! Appa! Walk slowly! I can't keep up with you!” cried the boy Veeramalai.

“Only your name is Veeramalai! Can't you even walk fast?” Chozhan Thotti laughed teasingly and then gave the boy an affectionate pat on the back.

As Chozhan Thotti went from village to village beating the drum and making the announcement, poor people began gathering by the thousand and two thousand at Maniyangurichi, also called Aarichampatti, both to receive food grain and to witness the wedding of Thamarai Naachchiyar, daughter of Malaikkozhundha Gounder.

To the delight of those gathered in the food-grain pavilion, a group of young women came toward the platform. Like a full moon among stars, Thamarai Naachchi swayed like a golden chariot and stepped onto the platform spread with a jewel-like carpet. Seeing her radiant beauty, the old women standing in line in the pavilion opened their toothless mouths as if they had already seen Rathi herself and praised and blessed her: “My golden girl! You look just like Rathi, child!” Even elderly men forgot the weariness of their age and stood like trees staring at Thamarai Naachchi, unaware even if a fly entered their open mouths. And what need be said of young hearts? Many of the young men in the line thought with a sigh, “Even being poor has given us one good chance. Otherwise, could we receive food grain today from the hands of this great beauty? On the pretext of receiving it, if we can gaze at her without taking our eyes away for a while, what need is there afterward for the state of Vaikuntha or the state of Sivaloka?”

The distribution of food grain began. People came one by one to the platform. “Kambu? Varagu? Cholam? Rice?” one of Thamarai Naachchi's friends would ask. Each person would state a preference.

<!-- source boundary: scan 21 → scan 22 -->

One friend would take that grain and place it in Thamarai's hand. Thamarai would give it away. “Amma, may you live like a queen!” the old women said. “May you receive all sixteen and live a great life!” elderly men blessed her, touching her head. Only her body was present on that pavilion platform; her face made it unmistakably clear that her mind was wandering elsewhere. In eyes white as conch shells, her two pupils fluttered like black beetles without wings with which to fly.

The sound of several horse-drawn carriages entering the village at the same time could be heard. Even as she continued distributing grain, Thamarai Naachchi listened intently to that swell of sound.

All the carriages were covered with gleaming silk curtains. There could not have been fewer than a hundred. In those carriages came Chellaatha Gounder of Nelli Valanaadu and his close relatives for the wedding. Only the carriage carrying the groom Maanthiyappan had been decorated almost like a chariot and was drawn by four horses. Wearing a turban of gold zari, with an ornament set beautifully at its edge with diamonds, pearls and coral, and covered in priceless jewellery, the groom Maanthiyappan sat in that chariot-like carriage, smiling grandly as he thought of the wedding to take place the next day.

At the entrance to the village, with *perigai* drums, war drums, conches, trumpets and other drums sounding, Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu, on behalf of Malaikkozhundha Gounder, gave all those decorated carriages a lavish welcome. Chellaatha Gounder introduced his son Maanthiyappan to Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu, and the two embraced warmly. The carriages then moved in procession through the streets of Maniyangurichi.

<!-- source boundary: scan 22 → scan 23 -->

Seeing the wedding celebration blaze into splendour beyond anything one could ignore, the whole village floated in excitement.

At the pavilion where Thamarai Naachchiyar was distributing food grain, it could be said that nearly three-fourths of the crowd had already dispersed. She too had become extremely tired and weary.

“What is this, Thamarai! You're already so tired today? Tomorrow's wedding is still ahead—and so is everything that comes after it. They say the bridegroom is terribly rough—what are you going to do then?”

a mischievous friend asked softly.

In her distress, Thamarai could not enjoy her friend's teasing. She flared up sharply, and “Just keep quiet!” sprang from her.

Young men came one by one between elderly men and women to receive grain from Thamarai. Because they did not move away quickly, the distribution was proceeding only slowly.

“Even at this young age, they are suffering in poverty,” Thamarai said softly to one of her friends.

“If they're too lazy to work, this is how they deserve to suffer!” the friend muttered.

Then a young man came forward to receive grain, wearing a ragged, torn garment that looked as though it had been soaked in a drain, with dishevelled hair and a beard, and without even the strength to walk. His inability to walk did not mean he was an old man. Nor did he appear ill. Thamarai Naachchiyar looked with deep pity at the young man, who seemed as though he had gone hungry for many days. He too looked intently at her.

Just then, asking, “What is it, Thamarai, Amma? Still not finished?” Thamarai Naachchi's

<!-- source boundary: scan 23 → scan 24 -->

mother Perumayi Ammal arrived there. The starving beggar caught her eye.

“What do you want, son—rice? Kambu? Varagu?” Perumayi Ammal herself asked him. The utterly destitute young man instantly sang out an answer no one there expected.

“I did not come for kambu, cholam or varagu, Aththai—  
I came to tie the marriage thaali  
around your daughter's neck as her husband, Aththai!”

Perumayi Ammal was jolted by this startling answer.

“Hey! What did you say?” she roared, moving toward him. Before she could reach him, the young man removed his dishevelled hair and beard, cast off his disguise, looked at them and said:

“You tease me as a Machchasaami  
who doesn't know anything—now how is  
this Machcha Gounder's trick?”

He laughed foolishly. Paying no heed to the presence of her mother, the friends surrounding her or the people gathered to receive grain, Thamarai Naachchiyar cried aloud, “Aththaan!” and embraced him.

For the first time in his life, the young man was experiencing a pleasure he had never known in Thamarai's embrace when whip lashes fell across his back with sharp cracks. “Appa! Let him go!” Thamarai cried and tried to intervene, but she could not. “Hey, Nelliyangodan! Have you come by a crooked route to ruin my family? If I don't rip out your guts and wear them as a garland, my name isn't Malaikkozhundha Gounder!” roared the major landholder of Maniyangurichi, lunging at the young man and thrashing him with the whip. Unable to bear the sight, Thamarai fainted with “Aththaan! Aththaan!”, 

<!-- source boundary: scan 24 → scan 25 -->

ringing out in her scream! The young man lay on the ground like a withered lily stalk. Still crying out, Thamarai watched as Malaikkozhundha Gounder ordered that he be taken away and locked in a shed where *karaiyaan* and *sellu* insects could bite him. Four men carried the unconscious young man away and shut him inside that dreadful shed.

“Chellaatha Gounder need not know any of this. Take Thamarai away at once and have her treated,” Malaikkozhundha Gounder told his wife, and hurried out.
