5

Thamarai Naachchi's Vow

Thamarai Naachchiyar stood staring without taking her eyes from the direction in which her elder brother Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu had gone. Most of the relatives seemed unwilling even to stay for the wedding feast and were dispersing from the pavilion. Only a few relatives, wishing to respect the custom called *moikkaalari*, made their *moi* offering and left after taking leave. The groom's brother-in-law was expected to remain nearby until all the wedding events were completed and until the *arumaikkarar* performed *arugumanam* and gave their blessing. But after Nelliyangodan's brother-in-law Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu, caring nothing for any of that, had hurled words that struck his sister Thamarai's heart like a sledgehammer and gone away, what obligation remained for the other relatives? Some who felt sympathy stayed without immediately announcing their departure, thinking they might at least give the man of Maniyangurichi a little comfort. Those who had already been displeased by the sudden change of groom at the wedding frowned even more, criticised the situation among themselves, climbed into whatever vehicles they had brought and hurried back to their villages. Only a few who believed that it was not human to behave like birds that abandon a dried-up tank and flee when another person falls into distress remained in the wedding house, upholding the dignity of the word “relationship.”

<!-- source boundary: scan 52 → scan 53 -->

It was an important ceremony for the newly married couple to pour water over their parents' feet, wash them, scatter flowers and bow. The bride Thamarai performed *paadha poosai* for her father Malaikkozhundha Gounder and her mother Perumayi Ammal and rose. Tears poured from Nelliyangodan's eyes like a waterfall. His father Kolaatha Gounder and his mother Pavalaathaal were no longer there; they were not alive to bless him; he could not receive the good fortune of performing *paadha poosai* for them. Longing, grief and sorrow pierced through his heart.

When one thought of the son of Kolaatha Gounder—who had turned Nelli Valanaadu into a land overflowing with prosperity, spread his influence and lived in prominence—now standing diminished and weakened in a humiliating situation, unable to show it openly and drooping in shame, could it be surprising that his eyes became waterfalls?

Folding his hands before Malaikkozhundha Gounder and Perumayi Ammal, Nelliyangodan said in a trembling voice, “I have lost my mother and father. From now on, you are my mother and father!” His father-in-law and Aththai were moved. Perumayi was plainly struggling to contain the anguish in her heart, unable to speak. It was also clear that Malaikkozhundha Gounder had been deeply affected by the distress that everything had happened beyond his control. Silently sighing, Perumayi Ammal embraced her daughter Thamarai and wiped away the tears rolling down her cheeks like dew gathered on the tips of morning grass. But the tears only welled up like a spring.

Even in that condition, the women relatives and elders continued to conduct some of the wedding rites without interruption.

New footwear prepared for the newly married couple was brought and placed on a three-legged stool set at the street entrance. With auspicious music sounding, the couple were led

<!-- source boundary: scan 53 → scan 54 -->

there and made to put on the footwear. An iron ladle was placed in the bride Thamarai's hand and she was made to push a mound of rice on a plate toward the groom Nelliyangodan with it, while the women relatives standing around them joked and teased with delight. Neither the couple nor the man of Maniyangurichi nor his wife could take part in those lively events with their hearts in them, but the ceremonies were completed for form's sake.

After all this, the couple were taken from the pavilion into the house and seated.

Perumayi Ammal suffered because she could not take pride and delight in the wedding of her only daughter. That mother had never expected her son Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu to behave so uncivilly in the wedding pavilion. Like someone in an age when even crying aloud “Ayyo, Amma!” under the crack of whip blows was treated as an offence, enduring the pain and merely twisting his body, Perumayi Ammal bent and yielded while continuing to attend to the wedding-related duties. She was with the couple when they were brought into the house and seated. Malaikkozhundha Gounder was not there even then. Perumayi Ammal's eyes kept circling around as though asking, “Where is my husband?”

At that very moment, Malaikkozhundha Gounder was standing near the wedding chamber in the pavilion and reading with agitation a letter that had come to him bearing the heading “Urgent.” A shadow of fear had settled on his face. Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu stood nearby, looking alternately at his father and the palm-leaf letter, making it certain that he must have been the one who had brought the letter to him.

It was a letter from Thalaiyur Kaali! Malaikkozhundha Gounder's hands trembled as he read it.

“To the respected man of Maniyangurichi, written by Thalaiyur Kaali, minor ruler of Menaadu: I have no personal hurt that I was humiliated by you in your village. I do not consider it important. But on my account, Chellaatha Gounder of Valanaadu, a friend of my country and personally my patron, has been deeply wounded. You do not fully know the friendship between him and me, nor the extent to which my country needs the support of the region under his rule. The territory under my rule is rich in forests. The Valanaadu territory under Chellaatha Gounder's authority is rich in fertile land. We have trained the people of my region to be warlike. After retaining what they need, we provide the remaining weapons of war to Valanaadu. In return, we receive from Valanaadu the food grains needed by our Menaadu. Such a relationship has existed between our two countries. When my father, King Periya Kaali, died, I was very young. Even while he was alive, he had entrusted Chellaatha Gounder with the responsibility of raising me and making me fit to rule. Having grown up from childhood under Chellaatha Gounder and learned many experiences of government under his protection, I accept him as my patron. It was because Nelliyangodan possessed not even a little of the strength, capacity, knowledge or clarity needed to protect after Kolaatha Gounder the territory that had been under Kolaatha Gounder's authority and the people living there that I greatly helped in transferring that region from him to Chellaatha Gounder's control. Today Chellaatha Gounder governs Valanaadu through his own statecraft and abundance of intelligence. The marriage that was to take place between your daughter Thamarai and Chellaatha Gounder's son Maanthiyappan might have created not only a new relationship between you and Chellaatha Gounder but even a new friendship between you and me. Through some working of fate, it could not take place. Now the suspicion that has arisen in Chellaatha Gounder's mind and mine

<!-- source boundary: scan 55 → scan 56 -->

cannot be treated lightly. We believe that there is great calculation and scheming involved in making Nelliyangodan your son-in-law. That is to say: whatever Chellaatha Gounder thinks, I too think. To put it briefly, you have not merely given your daughter to your son-in-law Nelliyangodan. You are going to help him recover the land he lost. Only then can your daughter Thamarai Naachchiyar live like a queen. If Thamarai Naachchiyar is to live in that manner, must Nelliyangodan not recover Nelli Valanaadu from Chellaatha Gounder and become the minor ruler of that region? Our settled assessment is that this was why the groom was suddenly changed at the wedding according to a plan. You may deny this view. If that denial is genuine, you must demonstrate it through your actions. Otherwise, I must take it that you are planning to confront Chellaatha Gounder of Valanaadu in support of Nelliyangodan. If such opposition arises against my patron, Thalaiyur Kaali's forces cannot merely stand and watch. Rather than protecting after danger arrives, I will have to act in accordance with protecting before it comes. The first stage of that action will be for Thalaiyur Kaali's army to lay siege to Maniyangurichi and seize it. If you wish to avoid such a difficult situation, you must show in action that your role ended with conducting the marriage of Nelliyangodan and Thamarai and that you will give him no further support. Whether you take this letter as a request or as a warning is up to you.” Thus it was signed “Thalaiyur Kaali” and bore the seal of the minor kingdom of Menaadu. After reading the long letter, the man of Maniyangurichi writhed as though made to stand in fire.

“Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu! What you said has come true! Danger has come to Maniyangurichi itself!” he said in shock, gripping his son's shoulder.

“Appa! You ignored what I said! Listening to Amma and the girl, they have reduced the future of Maniyangurichi to dust! Thalaiyur Kaali is the minor ruler of Menaadu! He is one who never crosses the line drawn by Chellaatha Gounder! How can we live after making enemies of both him and the Gounder of Valanaadu?” Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu said.

“Come, come with me!” Malaikkozhundha Gounder said, putting an arm around his son's shoulder and taking him into the house.

Seeing the two of them, Thamarai forgot that she had been seated beside the groom Nelliyangodan among the women relatives for the *nalangiduthal* ceremony. “Anna! Appa!” she cried joyfully and sprang up to run toward them. Before her brother Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu could even open his mouth, Thamarai herself began speaking rapidly.

“Anna, why are you so angry with me? Why did you say all that in front of everyone and leave? Do I not even have the right to make a good decision about my own life, Anna? What wrong did I do by marrying the man I had chosen in my heart as my husband all my life? Why did you humiliate me like that in the wedding pavilion, Anna?”

Thamarai Naachchiyar wept aloud. She sobbed. Gripping his feet tightly, she wept uncontrollably. Her mother Perumayi Ammal lifted her to her feet, told her to stop crying and tried to comfort her.

“What is this, Thamarai? It is only your elder brother who became angry—what of it? Can water pull away and water separate? He did not like this wedding, that is all; would he abandon his younger sister for that? Do you

<!-- source boundary: scan 57 → scan 58 -->

know how lovingly your brother has raised you from the day you were born?” Even as Perumayi tried to console Thamarai, Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu cut in, shouting with anger overflowing, “Amma! Why all those old stories? Here is Appa; he is going to tell a new story. Listen to that!” Nelliyangodan, who until then had been seated on a low board for the *nalangu* ceremony, sprang up in alarm.

“I am not saying anything! Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu, you tell them everything yourself!” The man of Maniyangurichi lifted the burden and placed it on his son's head. Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu, having hardened his heart enough not to regard it as a great burden, came close to Thamarai and told her certain things with emphasis, clarity and finality.

“We raised you in splendour, laying you in a silver cradle and placing a golden rattle in your hand. Now you are going to leave this house. Where are you going? That is the pain! You are going somewhere with a wanderer who has neither house nor home. Never mind; it is the heaven you chose for yourself. Everything that must be given to you in the name of *seervarisai* has been stacked at the entrance: gold plates, silver pots, a gold feeding cup edged with diamonds for use when you have a child, an ivory cot, chairs set with navaratna gems, a silk mattress—every kind of *seervarisai* has been piled up. Take them and you and your husband must leave this house this very moment. But one thing: you yourselves must arrange the carts and vehicles to carry that *seervarisai*. There is another important difficulty—you have no place of your own in which to take all those costly *seervarisai* objects and display them. Poor things; I pity you! Never mind! I say this on Appa's behalf: you and your husband are leaving here now.”

While he was speaking, Perumayi Ammal fell at her husband's feet and asked, “Thambi! What are you saying? And you—do you agree to this too?”

“My son is speaking with my consent,” Malaikkozhundha Gounder said.

“Appa!” Thamarai screamed.

“There is no longer any place in this house for you or your husband! Every bond of affection between you and us has burned to ashes!” Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu shouted.

Thamarai Naachchiyar became like a woman possessed.

“Anna, today you may drive us out—it does not matter. Keep here all the *seervarisai* you have so generously given, because, as you mocked us, we do not have a house in which to keep it. But one thing, Anna: before you, my brother, I swear upon the mother who gave birth to me, and I swear upon my father. You said the bonds of affection end today. They certainly will not! They never can! My brother who mocked me saying I have no house in which to keep the *seervarisai*—you are going to see my Aththaan and me living in a many-storeyed mansion! My dear brother who said our bonds of affection have burned away—you are going to come and beg me to give your daughter in marriage to the lion-cub son I will bear!” Thamarai vowed in a loud voice.

“If you are going to give birth, give birth to two lion cubs! I will bring both of them and tie them like dogs at the entrance of my house to stand guard.”

That was how Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu mocked her.

“I will bear them, Anna, I will! You too will have two daughters, and I will feast my eyes on the sight of you coming in search of those lions,” Thamarai swore again, and said, “Come,

<!-- source boundary: scan 59 → scan 60 -->

Aththaan, let us go!” Gripping Nelliyangodan's hand, she pulled him along and left.

Malaikkozhundha Gounder and his son Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu stood watching them go.

Neither of them noticed that Perumayi Ammal had fainted and collapsed onto the mat below.

Crossing the outer entrance, Thamarai and Nelliyangodan walked on foot far away from the house of the major landholder of Maniyangurichi.

The *seervarisai* objects of gold, silver and ivory that had been given to Thamarai lay unattended at the entrance of the Maniyangurichi house.
