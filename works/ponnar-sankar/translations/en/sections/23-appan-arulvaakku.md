20

The Father's Oracle

The past events Maayavar had described, and the incident in which Raakkiyannan rescued the children who were about to disappear, had first been told by Kundrudaiyaan and Thamarai Naachchiyar to Maayavar, and he had now retold them. Thus Kundrudaiyaan and Thamarai Naachchiyar, who had until then shown only the reflection of their own feelings bound up with those events, now waited with great eagerness to hear what Maayavar was going to say next.

Raakkiyannan had intercepted the midwives who were carrying away the children to kill them according to Chellaatha Gounder's conspiracy. Concealing himself beneath the black cloak so he could not be recognised, he saved the children. Then he took leave, asking that no one reveal who had rescued them.

Having described everything up to that point, Maayavar looked at those present and said, “From now on, what I am going to tell you consists of truths I learned from Raakkiyannan himself when I met him at his training camp in Maarik Goundan Paalayam.” With that preface, he began.

“The midwives sent to carry away the children and kill them were immediately confined in the underground prison at Sankaranmalai. The midwives did not know that they had been caught because Raakkiyannan had intervened. According to Chellaatha—

<!-- source boundary: scan 191 → scan 192 -->

—Gounder's plan, a Valanaadu warrior wrapped in a black cloak had been waiting, ready to receive the children. Raakkiyannan killed that warrior, covered himself with the cloak and stood in his place. Believing him to be the Valanaadu warrior who had been arranged to wait there, the midwives were deceived. After the two midwives had been sent to the underground prison, Raakkiyannan revealed himself to Kundrudaiyaan and Thamarai Naachchiyar. When he took leave, he warned them, ‘Be careful! Danger may come to these children at any time!’

“What use is there in blaming the arrow when the archer is still there?” This question arose in the minds of both Kundrudaiyaan and Thamarai. A few days later, the two went down to the underground chamber and met the imprisoned midwives.

Although well-cooked, tasty food was being given to them, the midwives complained that it was not enough for their ravenous hunger. Kundrudaiyaan immediately ordered the prison guard to arrange for food to be given until the midwives themselves said it was enough. Ashamed by the guilt of having come to kill the children of such a good, open-hearted man, they lowered their heads. A special respect grew in them for Kundrudaiyaan, who stood as an example of the Kural saying, ‘Punish those who have done you harm by doing them good until they are ashamed.’ Both fell at the feet of Kundrudaiyaan and Thamarai Naachchiyar and cried for forgiveness. The midwives explained that everything had been Chellaatha Gounder's conspiracy. Kundrudaiyaan pardoned them and released them from the underground prison. Seeing the midwives tremble in fear that Chellaatha Gounder might harm them, Thamarai Naachchiyar gave them enough gold and sent them away to flee to another country and survive.

<!-- source boundary: scan 192 → scan 193 -->

“There was a young man who carried all this news to Raakkiyannan. His name was Veeramalai. That boy, who was training in Raakkiyannan's camp, frequently travelled to Sankaran Malai and Kudaiyur on Raakkiyannan's orders, gathered news about the children and brought it back to him. The boy Veeramalai kept troubling his mind over why Raakkiyannan cared so deeply about those children.

“Raakkiyannan knew that Veeramalai was confused in this way. Yet he did not think it necessary to give him any explanation. Raakkiyannan's principle was that one must go out of one's way to support innocent people like Kundrudaiyaan, who considered it a great sin even to think in one's heart of causing harm to anyone.

“‘Today they have had expensive silk jackets stitched for the children.’

“‘Today two golden cradles have been made and hung for the children.’

“‘Today pearl garlands were placed upon the children.’

“‘Today diamond-studded garlands were fastened upon the children.’

“From time to time Veeramalai kept giving Raakkiyannan such reports.

As Maayavar said this, Veeramalai's heart softened. Yet he did not depart even slightly from his resolve not to reveal his identity; he listened as though Maayavar were speaking about some other young man. Ponnar and Sankar looked at him, inwardly admiring Veeramalai's firmness. They marvelled at his resolve.

<!-- source boundary: scan 193 → scan 194 -->

Everyone was eager to know what words would next come from Maayavar's mouth. Maayavar continued.

“One day an *olai* came to Raakkiyannan from Thalaiyur. Because it had come from the Thalaiyur palace, an important warrior of the palace had brought it. Was Raakkiyannan not the teacher who had trained Thalaiyur Kaali too in archery, swordsmanship and other arts? So the *olai* had not been sent casually from the Thalaiyur palace, but with due honour. The Kaali temple, the clan deity of the Thalaiyur royal family, had undergone major renovation at enormous cost, and the *olai* was an invitation to the temple's *kumbabhishekam*. Raakkiyannan wrote a reply thanking them for the invitation and sent it back with the Thalaiyur warrior. As the *olai* had stated, magnificent arrangements were made for the *kumbabhishekam* of the Thalaiyur Kaaliyamman temple. The priest of the Kaali temple, a merchant named Sembakulan, stood at the forefront and supervised the festival arrangements. As far as that temple was concerned, Sembakulan was priest, trustee and manager all in one. The Thalaiyur kings had entrusted all responsibility for their clan-deity Kaali temple to Sembakulan's family. Sembakulan, who came in that hereditary line, was engaged in the gold trade and at the same time held an important position in the temple administration. Since the temple renovation and the entire *kumbabhishekam* responsibility had been entrusted to him, he had written false accounts for more than half the money and swallowed it himself.

“That Sembakulan, who stole even in the renovation of the deity's own temple, staged the *kumbabhishekam* festival in a manner that left spectators awestruck, precisely to conceal his plunder. Raakkiyannan too accepted the invitation and went to Thalaiyur for the *kumbabhishekam*. Thalaiyur Kaali welcomed and hosted him with great respect as his teacher. Before him—

<!-- source boundary: scan 194 → scan 195 -->

—Chellaatha Gounder, patron of Thalaiyur Kaali, and his son Maanthiyappan had already arrived in Thalaiyur. Separate guest houses had been allotted to Chellaatha Gounder and Maanthiyappan. There was a reason father and son were not made to stay in the same guest house. Since Thalaiyur Kaali and Maanthiyappan were young men of roughly the same age, the arrangement allowed the two to sit privately together and drink liquor. On the night before the *kumbabhishekam*, Raakkiyannan himself had seen that revelry with his own eyes. His guest house was next to theirs, and so he had the opportunity. Before Thalaiyur Kaali had finished one cup of liquor, Maanthiyappan had apparently finished nine. Raakkiyannan also saw that Thalaiyur Kaali drank in moderation and tried, without success, to restrain Maanthiyappan. When Maanthiyappan became dazed and collapsed onto his bed, Thalaiyur Kaali left the room. What happened after that was an obscene spectacle. There was a beautiful statue of a woman in the room. To Maanthiyappan's intoxicated eyes, the statue seems to have appeared as a living woman. In his imagination, the statue danced, swayed at the sight of him, and fell languidly upon his shoulder. Swept along by that fantasy, he embraced the statue and began fondling it in drunken frenzy, until at last he and the statue fell together onto the floor. Seeing this, Raakkiyannan's disgust toward Chellaatha Gounder's family increased another measure.”

When Maayavar mentioned Maanthiyappan's bestial frenzy, Arukkaani Thangam could not help recalling the cruel act Maanthiyappan had committed toward her.

She could not forget the brutal incident in which Maanthiyappan, seized by a degradation found nowhere else in Kongu Mandalam—an ignoble thought that, she felt, would not arise even in a man afflicted with mental illness—

<!-- source boundary: scan 195 → scan 196 -->

—had chased her. Arukkaani Thangam sighed, thinking that whether or not human qualities arose among animals, a few people with animal qualities certainly arose among humans, and Maanthiyappan was an example. Her lips murmured that he was the axe-handle born to destroy the pride of the Kongu Vellalar clan.

Maayavar continued giving the details Raakkiyannan had told him.

“Dawn came. Thalaiyur Kaali arrived to take his friend to the *kumbabhishekam*. His friend Maanthiyappan, however, was fast asleep embracing the statue. In his anger, Thalaiyur Kaali had a pot of water poured over him. Once Maanthiyappan's drunkenness cleared, Thalaiyur Kaali set out with him, went to the guest house where Chellaatha Gounder was staying and took him along, and then went to the guest house where Raakkiyannan was staying and took him too. After that they all went to the *kumbabhishekam*.

“With Sembakulan supervising everything, the *kumbabhishekam* was completed. Afterwards Thalaiyur Kaali, Raakkiyannan, Chellaatha Gounder and Maanthiyappan went to the sanctum hall of the Kaaliyamman temple. Sembakulan performed the worship for Kaaliyamman, offered incense, lamps and *naivedyam*, and came first toward King Thalaiyur Kaali with sacred ash, kumkum and other *prasadam*. Suddenly, he began to dance as though possessed.

“‘Ah! The Goddess has possessed him! She is going to speak some oracle! Mother! Speak, Mother, speak! Give the King of Thalaiyur a good word, Mother!’ Chellaatha Gounder cried in fear and devotion, embracing Sembakulan.

Sembakulan, dancing in possession, shouted, “I cannot speak a good word! A great danger threatens my child!”

<!-- source boundary: scan 196 → scan 197 -->

“‘Which child, Mother? What danger, Mother? Tell us, Kaali Devi!’ Chellaatha Gounder asked in agitation.

“‘I have only one child—this King of Thalaiyur here! It is this child who is in danger!’

Hearing this, the King of Thalaiyur himself was overwhelmed by shock. He stood distressed, wondering whether they had made some error in matters concerning Kaaliyamman.

“‘Two children have been born in Kundrudaiyaan's mansion to destroy the Thalaiyur family. If those children grow into young men, they will bring destruction—utter destruction—to the lineage of Thalaiyur Kaali!’

Sembakulan's eyes flashed as terrifyingly as the eyes of the Kaali image itself. Believing Kaali Devi herself stood before him, the King of Thalaiyur spoke in a trembling voice with great humility:

“‘Mother! Kaaliyamma! How can that danger be prevented? You yourself show the way, Mother! I will make and place upon you a golden armour!’ he said, bowing. Sembakulan, who had wholly become Kaali Devi, said:

“‘Sit with your close friends and consult! Do not forget that the man who fails to pluck out the root while it is still a sprout is a fool!’ Saying this, he spun round and round, flinging sacred ash and kumkum, and fell to the floor. Saying the Goddess had departed from him, Chellaatha Gounder and Maanthiyappan lifted Sembakulan and comforted him.

The King of Thalaiyur left the Kaali temple with a worried face, climbed into his chariot and went to the palace.

Raakkiyannan suspected that there was some mystery in Sembakulan's apparent possession by Kaali. Pretending to leave the temple, he instead withdrew behind a stone pillar there—

<!-- source boundary: scan 197 → scan 198 -->

—and watched. Then Chellaatha Gounder took out a bag and held it toward the priest Sembakulan. ‘Here, Sembakula! You performed the drama exactly as I told you. Here are a hundred sovereigns, just as I promised!’ he said in praise, affectionately patting his back and handing him the bag.

That evening after the *kumbabhishekam*, King Kaali, Chellaatha Gounder, Raakkiyannan and Maanthiyappan gathered in Thalaiyur Kaali's council hall.

“‘What danger can children cause you? There is no need to be disturbed over it,’ Raakkiyannan told the King of Thalaiyur. Chellaatha Gounder immediately interrupted and rejected that view.

“‘Can you dismiss them simply because they are children? Was Krishna not born to kill Kamsa? If that Krishna had been properly identified and killed while he was still an infant, Kamsa need not have died!’

That was Chellaatha Gounder's argument. In the end, King Thalaiyur Kaali too accepted that the argument was strong.

“‘All right. Then who is to kill those children?’

That was the King of Thalaiyur's question. Raakkiyannan answered it at once.

“‘King of Thalaiyur! If the two children of Kundrudaiyaan pose a danger to the life of you, my student, then I myself will kill those children.’

Seeing that his own teacher Raakkiyannan had come forward to save him, Thalaiyur Kaali fell at his feet and bowed.

Then the suggestion made by Chellaatha Gounder startled Raakkiyannan.

<!-- source boundary: scan 198 → scan 199 -->

“‘The children must be brought here and handed over to the King of Thalaiyur himself. The King of Thalaiyur must personally carry out the task of sacrificing them before Kaali!’

When Chellaatha Gounder said this, the Thalaiyur king nodded and said, ‘Yes, that is right.’ To Raakkiyannan, it felt as though a thousand hammer blows had fallen upon his head.”

As Maayavar said this and wiped his troubled eyes, everyone listening to him became motionless.
