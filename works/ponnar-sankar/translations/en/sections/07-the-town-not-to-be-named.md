6

The Town Not to Be Named

Driving away the pitch darkness with his radiant hands, the sun rose in the eastern sky. The beauty of the sky glowing gold joined with the beauty of the overflowing Amaravathi River, turning nature's awakening into a delightful sight.

The speed of the Amaravathi made it seem eager to see the Kaveri, rejoice in her and mingle with her. The fronds of the tall coconut trees along the Amaravathi bank swayed in the gentle morning breeze. In the rich green fields beside the river, tiny fish came through the irrigation sluices and leapt about. Men, helped by boys, cast nets into the river and caught fish. Women filled the baskets they had brought with the fish caught in the nets. Beyond the places where the nets were cast, gleaming *kendai* fish leapt in midstream and near the opposite bank; sunlight struck them and made them look like sheets of silver, a sight that stole the heart. Farmers moved along the field bunds carrying tools such as hoes as they went to attend to their work. The roar of the swollen Amaravathi and the sounds of the southern-country songs sung by fishers and farmers as they carried out their tasks seemed to form a welcoming hymn for the sunrise.

From far away on the north bank of the river came the sound of a horse approaching from the west. The farmers and fishers turned to look in that direction and then returned to their work. From this it was clear that people often travelled on horseback along that bank and that no one considered the approaching horse remarkable.

A man sat on the horse, feasting his eyes on the Amaravathi hurrying along as though scooping up fallen flowers from the riverside trees to scatter over Mother Kaveri, and on the abundance of coconuts hanging in bunches from the tall palms by the bank. He was neither a tottering old man nor, for that matter, a young man. He appeared to be between fifty-five and sixty. A moustache and beard mixed with white and black gave his face a radiant, almost ascetic look. Though he wore a pure white robe, he was not without leggings suited to horseback riding. He had a long, curved, prominent nose and sharp eyes. His hair had grown down past the nape and touched his shoulders. His body was well built. He did not race the horse against the Amaravathi; as far as speed was concerned, he seemed to have left the choice to the horse. But surely it was he who had to decide the destination. It was because he came with such a purpose that his gaze kept moving toward those working in the fields beside the river and those fishing in it.

When he reached the vicinity of a riverside village called Oththamaanthurai, he drew in the reins and stopped the horse.

“Ade! Is that Maayavar Ayya? What brings you at this hour?” a farm woman asked eagerly as she came from the field toward the bank. She clearly knew Maayavar, who had arrived on horseback. Though age had taken one or two of the front teeth of that farming woman,

her speech was still firm and clear.

“Oh, Vaangalayi? Are you well?” Maayavar asked as he dismounted from the horse.

Kolaatha Gounder had risen to influential leadership in Thirukkaampuliyur and later extended his authority into the Karur and Vaangal regions. Because he renovated the Amman temple at Vaangal and conducted its *kudamuzhukku* festival splendidly, and because he made the Vaangal region a *kaani* holding for the people of his Perungkudi group, people began calling him by the epithet “Vaangalaan.” In memory of “Vaangalaan,” men and women were respectively given names such as “Vaangili” and “Vaangalayi.” The farming woman who had hurried over on seeing Maayavar on horseback was one such woman bearing that name, Vaangalayi.

“Will you have some hot porridge this morning? That is all I can offer! You come from the palace—I don't know whether you would eat it.”

Vaangalayi not only asked with affection; she also held out to him the earthen pot of porridge she carried.

“I was riding along wondering what I might get to put some warmth and energy into me in this chill. The right food at the right time. Give it here! Ade... the pot itself is nicely hot. The porridge must be hot too!”

Maayavar drank all the porridge and handed the pot back to Vaangalayi. Her face blossomed with the satisfaction of having helped relieve another person's hunger with a pot of porridge.

“You wondered whether Maayavar, who comes from the palace, would drink porridge offered by this mud hut—is that not so? Do you understand Maayavar's nature now?”

he asked with a laugh.

“The whole world knows that your nature is golden! Am I going to discover it afresh now?” Vaangalayi replied.

“All right, all right, Vaangalayi! I came for something else. Which way must I go to reach Maarikkavundan Paalayam?”

“Are there villages Maayavar does not know? You ask me—are you teasing me?”

“No, Amma! It has been many years since I travelled through that side; that is why I ask. I have important work in Maarikkavundan Paalayam.”

“Go north, and before reaching the Kaveri bank turn a little southwest from Vaangal; that will take you to Maarikkavundan Paalayam.”

“All right, Amma; I am leaving. Thank you very much for this morning meal—hot and tasty, and beyond all that, given with the guileless heart befitting your Kongu Vellalar clan!” Maayavar said as he mounted the horse.

Overflowing with happiness, Vaangalayi showed shyness on her face even at that age. Lowering her head slightly, she looked at Maayavar and said, “Does only your Vettuvar clan have no guileless hearts? You yourself are an example of a heart white as a thumbai flower!”

“Amma, Vaangalayi! Only when you say that do I remember that I was born in the Vettuvar clan. Differences in the places people live, the work they do and the customs they follow created many divisions among human beings. In the course of time, a mistaken idea arose that those divisions were based on birth. That is why we have to identify me as Vettuvar clan and you as Vellalar clan. As far as I am concerned, everyone belongs to one clan. I am going, Amma!”

Maayavar urged the horse forward and it began racing toward Maarikkavundan Paalayam. Leaving Oththamaanthurai and crossing regions such as Idaikkaadu, it did not take him long to reach the boundary of Maarikkavundan Paalayam, about seven or eight *kal* from the Amaravathi riverbank. At the boundary he stopped the horse and looked around. Suddenly he saw a sight before him that he could not believe and stood stunned.

A herd of more than twenty fierce wild boars. In the middle of them stood three impressive young men. They had only *kattari* daggers in their hands. With their waistcloths drawn tight, all three held their daggers raised and faced the boars' attack. Unlike ordinary pigs wandering in streets, each animal had a body like a rhinoceros and similar strength, and the pack surrounded the three young men ferociously, lunging to knock them down. The three youths did not tire. No fear could be seen on their faces. Maayavar did not move his horse from there. The sight of only three young men fighting against the savage assault of the wild boars thrilled him.

Watching the maddened attack of those wild boars, Maayavar seemed to understand fully how wrong it was to dismiss them merely as pigs. He watched eagerly, eyes wide and unblinking. He understood that the young men were trying to tire the boars by brandishing their daggers and feinting as though they would stab and kill them. The boars did not seem likely to tire so easily. After a long time the terrible struggle came to an end. The youths' daggers plunged into the bellies of some boars and into the necks of others. They lifted some boars as though lifting black rocks and

<!-- source boundary: scan 65 → scan 66 -->

smashed them hard against the ground, flinging their bodies aside!

One or two boars ran off wildly as though grateful simply to have escaped alive. The three young men grasped one another by the shoulders, shook each other in congratulation and began walking toward a large well a little distance away to wash the dirt from their bodies.

Maayavar urged his horse in the direction they were walking. Seeing someone approaching on horseback, the three youths stopped. Looking at them, Maayavar asked, “Thambis! Where in this village is ‘Raakkiyannan's training camp’?” The three looked at one another and opened their eyes wide. Only one stepped forward and said, “Go straight this way... you will reach the camp.”

“Thank you very much, Thambi! I enjoyed watching the battle you fought with the boars. I praise your courage,” Maayavar said with a smile. The three youths looked him up and down once and exchanged some signal with their eyes. Then, without saying anything further to him, they resumed walking toward the well. Watching them with affection, Maayavar thought, “Ade! I let these brave young men go without learning their names!” He rode after them in the direction they were walking. “Thambis! May I know your names?” The three stood without answering. “Will you not tell me?” Maayavar asked again. Once more they looked at one another.

Instead of going to the well they had meant to reach, the youths suddenly turned in another direction and moved quickly along a narrow shortcut filled with stones and thorn bushes. Maayavar felt a great

<!-- source boundary: scan 66 → scan 67 -->

disappointment. Still seated on the horse, he watched all three until their figures disappeared. Then he guided the horse along the route one of the young men had indicated.

At another boundary of Maarikkavundan Paalayam, Maayavar rode into a large, secluded grove thick with trees. On one side, mango trees bore fruit in clusters. On another were many ilanthai trees. Elsewhere squirrels darted about the guava trees eating fruit. A high thorn fence surrounded the grove. As Maayavar entered, a voice sharply stopped him. “Who are you? Where are you entering without permission?” The young man who owned the voice stood before him with dignity. “I want to see Raakkiyannan,” Maayavar said with the calmness that seemed natural to him. “Seeing him can wait. First you must tell me who you are. Only then can I tell him and obtain his order whether you may be allowed in,” the youth replied with a touch of mischief. Smiling, Maayavar looked at him and said, “Thambi, tell him Maayavar has come.” “Is that one word enough?” the youth asked in return. “Everything is contained in it. Tell him that, and Raakkiyannan will come running to welcome me,” Maayavar said. A shadow of anger suddenly spread across the young man's face. “Ayya, what do you take our training-master Raakkiyannan for? Do you know that he is a lion of the Kongu Vellalar clan? Will that lion come running out to receive you?” the youth said heatedly. Maayavar studied him with a gentle smile and said, “Thambi! There is no doubt that Raakkiyannan is a Kongu lion. You are a young boy. You do not know the bond of affection between Raakkiyannan and me. Go, go and tell him Maayavar has come,” he said with affection.

<!-- source boundary: scan 67 → scan 68 -->

The youth did not go at once; hesitating, he slowly went inside.

A short while after the youth left, a man built like a *vengai* tree came running toward Maayavar. The moment Maayavar saw him, he leapt from the horse, ran forward and embraced him.

“Raakkiyanna, are you well?”

“I am well. And you?”

“I too am well.”

“Maayavar! How long it has been since I saw you... Aha... what a rare meeting this is... Come, let us go into the camp.”

With an arm over each other's shoulder, the two entered the camp as though swimming in a flood of joy. Raakkiyannan seated Maayavar on an ivory-inlaid chair and sat opposite him on another chair. Maayavar's eyes circled the training ground. Bows and arrows, spears, swords, striking staffs, *silambam* staffs, *kattari* daggers, shields and other weapons filled the camp. While Maayavar was looking at them with pleasure, the young man hurried over carrying two tender coconuts and placed one in each man's hand. Maayavar looked at him with a small smile; the youth shifted awkwardly.

“Maayavar! Please forgive this boy for behaving so strictly with you,” Raakkiyannan said.

“Why ask forgiveness? He is carrying out the task given to him with a sense of duty. Should we not praise him for that?”

Maayavar said, patting the youth on the back.

The two finished drinking the tender coconut water. The youth split the coconuts in two, scraped out the soft flesh inside and placed it on two plates,

<!-- source boundary: scan 68 → scan 69 -->

and gave them the plates. Maayavar and Raakkiyannan ate it with relish.

“Thambi! Why don't you have a tender coconut yourself?” Maayavar asked. The youth shook his head to say no and thanked him with a movement of the eyes for having asked. Maayavar looked at him with affection and asked, “Thambi, what is your name?” The youth looked closely at him, then looked at Raakkiyannan. Without answering Maayavar, he slowly moved away.

“What is this, Raakkiyannan! It looks as though this village might as well be named ‘The Town Not to Be Named,’” Maayavar said.

“Why?” Raakkiyannan asked.

“Whoever I ask refuses to tell me a name! Luckily I knew your name, so I could ask for you by name and make my way here,” Maayavar said.

Hearing that answer, Raakkiyannan burst into laughter so loud that the whole training camp seemed to shake.
