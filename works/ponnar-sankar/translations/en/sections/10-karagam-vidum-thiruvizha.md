8

The Festival of Releasing the Karagam

Leaving Maayavar astonished after he had repeatedly asked their names, the three young men—Ponnar, Sankar and Veeramalai Sambuvan—strode like lion cubs along a shortcut and reached the Vaangal Amman temple on the southern bank of the Kaveri. The river was in flood, touching both banks. The three young men saw the maiden Kaveri carrying in her wave-hands the flowers torn by the wind's hands from riverside trees and the blossoms that had flourished on plants and creepers, painting a picture with foam and froth; they also heard the magnificent music made by swarms of bees flying after the flowers drifting downstream. A verse from the *Silappathikaram* taught by their master Raakkiyannan came to mind, and they stood staring at the Kaveri without blinking.

“With bees at your sides humming splendidly,
clad in a garment of jewel-like flowers,
your dark fish-eyes opening and swaying,
you walked on—live long, Kaveri!”

They hummed the verse musically and expressed their delight.

Veeramalai Sambuvan, standing and gazing at the Vaangal Amman temple, remembered that his master Raakkiyannan had once told him the temple had been built during Kolaatha Gounder's rule. Neither Ponnar and Sankar nor Veeramalai Sambuvan

<!-- source boundary: scan 81 → scan 82 -->

knew that Kolaatha Gounder was Ponnar and Sankar's grandfather; that much was clear from the way they spoke about the temple.

“Ah, do you know how many villages worship the Amman in this temple? Not only on this bank of the river but on the other bank as well—the leading families who live in Marudhampatti, Namakkal, Vagurampatti, Vasanthapuram, Periyapatti, Vettaampadi, Muthukkaappatti, Saalaippaalayam, Pazhaiyapaalayam, Neykkaara-patti, Nallaiya Goundanpudhur, Erumappatti, Mahaarpatti and many other villages never fail to worship Vaangal Amman!”

Veeramalai Sambuvan thus expounded the temple's distinction. Being seven or eight years older than Ponnar and Sankar, he knew these details.

“Anna! Vaangal Amman temple has this kind of distinction on a historical basis, but they say the Madhukkarai Chellandiyamman temple on this same Kaveri bank has a legendary story behind it. Do you know it?” Sankar asked Ponnar eagerly.

“Thambi! If there were such a story, would our master not have told us?” Ponnar replied.

<!-- source boundary: scan 82 → scan 83 -->

Just then a voice called out, “That is a long story! I will tell it.” The three turned in surprise toward the direction from which the voice had come. It was the boatman's voice; he had spoken from a boat floating near the bank of the Kaveri.

“What are you saying, man?” Veeramalai Sambuvan went over toward the boatman.

“Today at the Madhukkarai Chellandiyamman temple there is a festival for releasing the karagam into the river. Every year, around *Pathinettam Perukku*, the festival takes place without fail. If you are interested, come and see it. We can go in my boat,” the boatman said, baring his teeth slightly as he looked at them.

“What is this festival of releasing the karagam?” Ponnar asked with interest.

“Can I tell you everything while you stand there on the bank? Take out your coins!” the boatman said in a firm tone.

“You will tell the story only if we pay you?” Veeramalai Sambuvan asked, showing a little anger.

“Today hundreds of men and women from all the villages will come to the Madhukkarai Chellandiyamman temple—some in carts, some on foot carrying bundles of packed rice, and some in boats like this. What a crowd and revelry there will be! Even the Kaveri beside the Chellandiyamman temple will be in an uproar. If you get into my boat and come to the temple, I will tell you the story while I row. If you want to come, take out the money!” Saying this, the boatman began to move the boat slowly.

Sankar felt an urge to learn about the karagam festival and the Chellandiyamman temple. So he looked at Ponnar and said, “Anna! Our master has given us leave today,

<!-- source boundary: scan 83 → scan 84 -->

has he not? Shall we go and come back by boat?” Ponnar did not feel it was proper to travel so far along the river without informing their master. He hesitated.

“No, Thambi! We can ask our master himself about the Chellandiyamman temple and learn from him. It is not right to go to another village without his permission!” he objected.

Veeramalai Sambuvan cut in. “The master will not be angry. Even if he says something, I will pacify him. Let us get into the boat now!” “Anna, you are only older than I am, but you have more impulse and haste than my younger brother! All right, all right—your two stubborn wills have won. I am coming too. Let us go!” Ponnar said. He was the first to get into the boat; Sankar and Veeramalai followed with smiles and sat down. Ponnar picked coins from the small purse tucked at his waist and placed them in the boatman's hand, and the boat left the Vaangal bank and set off through the swelling Kaveri toward Madhukkarai.

“Tell us the story of the karagam festival!” Sankar said.

“Here goes!” The boatman nodded his head and immediately began singing in a tune.

“First and foremost, O Vinayaka,
O son of the three-eyed Lord,
O Sakthi Ganapati,
O son of the good lady,
O elephant-faced one,
O sacred son of Hara!”

At the sound of the song Sankar tugged at the boatman and said, “Ayya! If you start the story from a hymn to Ganapati, dawn will break before you finish. Tell us the story of the karagam festival briefly!”

<!-- source boundary: scan 84 → scan 85 -->

“Is that so? All right, all right... I will tell you. Listen. In Kailasa there is a mountain called Naagamalai. A serpent there, having no child, performed penance to Paramasivan. That serpent had five heads. Moved by its penance, Paramasivan called Parvathi and ordered her, ‘Go and be born as a child to that serpent!’ Parvathi accordingly was born to the serpent as a girl. The serpent named the child Periya Kaandi and raised her. Do you know how it raised her?” The boatman began singing again:

“With five hoods spread, the serpent held a canopy;
beneath the serpent-canopy, a fine conch sang her lullaby.
In ever-changing colours, with beauty renewed each hour,
to the sound of the conch the mother herself raised the child.”

He stopped the song himself and continued the story.

“Periya Kaandiyamman, raised in this way, left Kollimalai for the Veeramalai forest. Crossing forests, hills and wild tracts, she came by way of Thottiyam and, while crossing the Kaveri bank, quarrelled with Mannudaiyaan. In the end he made a clay karagam for Periya Kaandiyamman and begged her forgiveness. Madhukkarai Chellandiyamman saw Periya Kaandi coming with the clay karagam. Chellandiyamman did not know that Parvathi Devi herself had been born as a girl from the serpent's womb and was coming as Periya Kaandi carrying a clay karagam. Chellandiyamman's karagam was a gold karagam. So that Amman looked at Periya Kaandiyamman and said:

‘With a gold karagam upon my head,
I rule as mistress of the earth;
who is this woman coming here
with a clay karagam on her head?
Is she greater than I am?
Is she the one of surpassing power?
Thus blocking the way, Chellandi
stood arguing her case.’

<!-- source boundary: scan 85 → scan 86 -->

“So a great rivalry arose between Periya Kaandiyamman and Chellandiyamman. The two of them made a wager.” The boatman's moustache fluttered as he spoke loudly.

“What wager?” The question could be seen in the eyes of all three young men.

“They would float the gold karagam in the river and the clay karagam too. Whichever karagam went all the way to Thiruppaarkadal, bathed in the sacred waters and returned—the woman to whom that karagam belonged would be the greater one. That was the wager! Accordingly, Periya Kaandiyamman released the clay karagam. Chellandiyamman released the gold karagam. Do you know what happened? The gold karagam went down into the river. The clay karagam went to Thiruppaarkadal, bathed there and returned. At once Chellandiyamman admitted that Periya Kaandiyamman was the greater one and asked her to call back and return the gold karagam. Periya Kaandi did exactly that and brought back the gold karagam. Chellandiyamman then said she would come with Periya Kaandi to the Veeramalai forest. ‘No. You remain here and continue bestowing grace on the people. I must go to the forest, perform penance and return to Kailasa,’ Periya Kaandiyamman said, and went away. It is this festival of releasing the karagam that they still celebrate every year in the Kaveri,” the boatman concluded.

“Did you see how much rivalry there is even among the deities?” Sankar said.

“Let rivalry and wagers be as they may, but there is a philosophy in this story. We must not forget to notice it!” Ponnar said.

All three, including the boatman, looked at his face to hear what he would say.

“The clay karagam won; the gold karagam lost. However simple it may be, a humble nature will always triumph. But display and the pride that comes from it

<!-- source boundary: scan 86 → scan 87 -->

must one day meet defeat. I think that is the philosophy this story explains,” Ponnar said.

The gleaming light that appeared in the eyes of Sankar and Veeramalai Sambuvan showed how delighted they were by his subtle thought. The boatman was not concerned about the philosophy. He opened the fold of cloth at his waist and counted how much he had earned that day while continuing to row. There were many signs that the boat was nearing the Chellandiyamman temple festival. Flowers had been wound around poles and planted high along the riverbank. All the boats floating near the temple were decorated and stood close to the bank. Men and women sat in them watching the festival. On the bank there were performances and entertainments—dummy-horse dancing, *silambam* displays and more. Instruments such as the *pambai* and *udukku* thundered loudly enough to deafen the ears. Countless *nayanam* pipes and *thavil* drums sounded in the Chellandiyamman festival pavilion.

Sankar looked at the boatman and asked, “Ayya, how do they conduct the festival of releasing the karagam?”

“Every year two new women are chosen—especially women from respectable families, and unmarried women at that. One dresses as Periya Kaandiyamman and the other as Chellandiyamman. They go down toward the river and release the clay and gold karagams in competition,” the boatman said.

“Is that so? Do the women actually enter such a deep river to release the karagams?” Veeramalai Sambuvan asked in surprise.

“No, no. They do not enter the water. They only make it look as though they do. Each gets into

<!-- source boundary: scan 87 → scan 88 -->

a separate boat. There must be no boatmen in those boats, because by then they are women upon whom the Amman's grace has descended. So each woman sits alone in a boat with her karagam. When the contest begins, the two women put the clay karagam and the gold karagam into the river. Then men secretly dive into the water, unseen by anyone, and bring out only the clay karagam,” the boatman said.

“Then the gold karagam remains in the river. Can anyone simply take it away?” Veeramalai Sambuvan asked.

“Oh! Ayya is tempted, is he? That cannot happen! According to the story, once Chellandiyamman asks, Periya Kaandiyamman has to bring that gold karagam back out for her!” the boatman said and laughed heartily. The boat in which they had come now reached the place where the festival was being held.

The music announcing the start of the festival, the sound of instruments, fireworks, and the devotees' cries of “Ambike! Ambike!” filled the air. Amid all this, two boats came gliding over the river like swans. In one, a woman held a clay karagam in her hands; in the other, a woman held a gold karagam. Seeing the two women adorned as Periya Kaandiyamman and Chellandiyamman, the festival crowds gathered on both banks of the river stood motionless with their mouths open. Among the people saying, “We have come to see this festival for so many years, but never in any year have such extraordinary beauties appeared adorned as Periya Kaandi and Chellandi,” another question arose: “Yes—who are these goddesses?”

Veeramalai Sambuvan asked the boatman the same question. The boatman thought for a moment and then, as if suddenly remembering, answered.

“They are the granddaughters of Malaikkozhundha Gounder of Maniyangurichi. He is no longer alive, of course. These

<!-- source boundary: scan 88 → scan 89 -->

two girls are the daughters of Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu Gounder.”

Before the boatman had finished giving this answer, a whirlwind blew as though it had come in a furious dance to uproot every tree in that region and carry them away.

The festival crowd erupted in cries and commotion. Many boats floating by the riverbank overturned, and those in them fell into the river and struggled. The boat carrying Ponnar and Sankar too was tossed in a great whirl. Realising it would capsize unless it was held upright against the force of the tossing water, Veeramalai Sambuvan suddenly jumped into the river and steadied the boat. In the confusion, the boatman fell into the water and was swimming.

Only Ponnar and Sankar remained in the boat. A terrifying sight unfolded before their eyes. The two boats in which the women dressed as Periya Kaandiyamman and Chellandiyamman were seated lost their balance under the force of the wind and were being swept away with the river. Ponnar and Sankar drove their own boat swiftly toward them.
