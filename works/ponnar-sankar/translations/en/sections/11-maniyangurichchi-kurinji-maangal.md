9

The Kurinji Deer of Maniyangurichi

“Muthayi! Pavalayi!” A middle-aged woman ran along the bank of the Kaveri crying out, heedless of the whirlwind. Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu ran along the bank behind her, shouting. Anyone who saw their agitation and wailing could easily understand that the people being swept down the river with their boats were the girls named Muthayi and Pavalayi, and that their parents were Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu and the woman flying ahead of him, Silambayi.

With the river in a bank-overflowing flood and the whirlwind joining it, the boats carrying Muthayi and Pavalayi were moving very fast eastward along the Kaveri. Perhaps because they were adorned as Periya Kaandiyamman and Chellandiyamman, the young women did not panic. With an attitude of letting whatever was to happen happen, they sat calmly and motionless in the boats. Just as they had boarded holding the clay karagam and the gold karagam in a flood of devotion, they remained seated with their eyes closed even as the whirlwind swept the boats away.

Ponnar and Sankar used their hands as oars to drive their own boat, trying at great speed to catch the boats of Muthayi and Pavalayi spinning ahead of them.

At one point, the force of the wind seemed to carry the two boats of Muthayi and Pavalayi toward the riverbank. Their parents, who had by then reached that part of the bank, approached the boats with one foot on the bank and one in the river, crying, “Ayyo! My daughters! My daughters!” But in the blink of an eye the whirlwind uprooted a large riverside tree and flung it down. The tree snapped with a tremendous sound and fell between the parents and the boats carrying Muthayi and Pavalayi. Had it missed by even a thread's breadth, both boats would have been smashed and those beautiful young women would have floated as corpses in the river. The force of the falling tree threw the river water upward; both boats rocked violently and once again began spinning out toward midstream, with no one there to save them.

Believing that they had sacrificed their treasures to the wind and the Kaveri, Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu and his wife struck their heads and wept. Seeing their parents suffering helplessly on the bank and a great crowd gathering around them, Muthayi and Pavalayi waved their hands, asking them not to panic. They also saw two young men in a boat rushing swiftly toward them.

The Kaveri's waves rose higher than a raging sea, and the whirling wind seemed to dance savagely as if ready to lift every boat somewhere away, hurl it down and smash it. In that setting, the boat carrying Ponnar and Sankar struggled against the current and fought the storm, moving to the right, left and ahead as they tried repeatedly to intercept the boats of Muthayi and Pavalayi until they were exhausted. Muthayi and Pavalayi appeared doubtful and afraid that the two men in the boat following and circling around them might be coming to cause them some further danger.

<!-- source boundary: scan 91 → scan 92 -->

Thinking that the women should not become frightened by such a thought, Ponnar and Sankar looked toward them and shouted loudly, “Do not be afraid! Do not be afraid!” Their cries reached the women's ears through the great roar of the whirlwind's furious breath. The women gripped their boats a little more tightly and made sure they did not fall into the river.

Having concluded that they could not save the women by remaining in their own boat, Ponnar and Sankar jumped out and began swimming. They thought they could swim to the two boats, stop them and save the women. No matter how hard the two swam, however, intercepting and stopping those boats did not seem easy.

But they were not going to turn back for that reason. Ponnar and Sankar kept swimming as though proving that their hearts had abundant room not only for courage but for compassion.

With their eyes fixed on the river in the hope that “those young men will save our two girls,” Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu and Silambayi kept running along the bank. Meanwhile Veeramalai Sambuvan swam ashore, joined them and ran while watching the turmoil in the river.

Maanthiyappan, who had come to see the festival, reached the scene dressed as usual in martial clothes like a warrior entering a battlefield, with a spear in his hand.

He had once come to marry Thamarai Naachchiyar and returned disappointed. He had no idea now that Ponnar and Sankar, the sons of Thamarai Naachchiyar, were struggling to save the daughters of Thamarai Naachchi's elder brother. Why, even Ponnar and Sankar themselves did not know that they were Thamarai

<!-- source boundary: scan 92 → scan 93 -->

Naachchi's children or know any of the details connected with it.

The youthful restlessness Maanthiyappan had shown when he came dressed as a bridegroom to marry Thamarai Naachchi was no longer there. Even so, he now had a well-developed, tightly built body, a thick moustache and an imposing appearance.

As soon as Prince Maanthiyappan, son of Chellaatha Gounder, the border-guarding ruler of Nelli Valanaadu, arrived, the crowd moved aside to make way for him.

When Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu saw Maanthiyappan, his eyes filled with tears. His lips trembled as though pleading, “Save my girls!” Nor was Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu entirely free of the doubt that Maanthiyappan might still be angry because his younger sister Thamarai Naachchi had not been married to him.

After Malaikkozhundha Gounder, Perumayi Ammal too passed away. Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu then took responsibility as the landholder of Maniyangurichi and established his authority in the Maniyangurichi region called Aarichampatti. Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu and Silambayi had married while his parents were still alive. Within four or five years of the wedding they became the parents of two daughters, Muthayi and Pavalayi, and a son, Vaiyam Perumaan. Later Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu's parents died one after the other.

Neither Thamarai Naachchi nor her husband was invited to any good or bad event that took place at Aarichampatti-Maniyangurichi. In keeping with the saying that not stepping across the threshold of those who do not respect you is worth a crore, Thamarai too never looked back at the house in which she had been born.

But Thamarai did not know that it was her own two sons who were now trying to save her elder brother's daughters struggling in boats on the Kaveri.

Ponnar and Sankar did not know that they themselves were the hard-won sons born to Thamarai.

Tormented by the thought that two living statues of beauty should not drown and die in the Kaveri, the two warriors fought the wind and the river's flood as they advanced toward the boats of Muthayi and Pavalayi.

“Little fellows! What can they do?” Maanthiyappan said with a dismissive laugh, not knowing who they were.

Before Maanthiyappan had even closed his mouth after saying this, Ponnar swam ahead of the boat in which Muthayi sat and stopped it. Following his elder brother, Sankar swam forward, was caught in a great whirlpool and escaped from it, then reached the front of the boat in which Pavalayi sat and stopped that one too.

Because the people in the boats were women, the two young men felt it would not be proper to climb aboard. They continued swimming and slowly pushed the boats toward the bank.

As the boat carrying Muthayi, which Ponnar was pushing, neared the bank, a great mountain snake that had been hanging among the aerial roots of a banyan tree rooted at the riverbank and spreading its branches over the water coiled itself around Ponnar's body. Ponnar could not let go of the boat. He had to keep swimming and bring it ashore. At the same time he had to fight the mountain snake. Ponnar did not falter. With one hand he held the boat; with the other he fought the snake; his legs kept swimming. The mountain snake coiled around Ponnar's entire body. Muthayi saw it and trembled in fear.

<!-- source boundary: scan 94 → scan 95 -->

The long, thick snake curled its whole body around Ponnar and fought with its wide mouth gaping, trying to force him inside. Ponnar fought back with great care, keeping the snake's head from coming near his own while at the same time preventing its slippery but powerful body from crushing him. Even while keeping Muthayi's boat from losing balance in the river, he used all his strength, yet he could not free himself from the mountain snake's tight, crushing grip. At one point the snake coiled around his neck as well and opened its mouth like a great cave, trying to swallow his face. Seeing this, Muthayi screamed and fainted inside the boat. At that moment Veeramalai Sambuvan, standing on the bank, took the *kattari* in his hand, aimed at the snake's head and threw it. The sharpness of the *kattari* and the speed with which it struck made the snake writhe and loosen its powerful grip, freeing Ponnar. As Ponnar began swimming again to bring Muthayi's boat ashore, another danger was waiting for Sankar.

A broad mouth clamped onto both of Sankar's legs as he swam pushing Pavalayi's boat. Sankar jerked his legs free. Blood gushed from them and reddened the Kaveri. Sankar had been caught by a crocodile. Seeing it, Pavalayi too collapsed unconscious in the boat. Sankar did not let go of the boat. He held it tightly and fought the crocodile. Is not a crocodile in water capable of resisting with the ferocity of a brute who holds a position of authority? It seemed that danger had come very close to Sankar's life.

For an instant Sankar released the boat and, in that brief interval, struck hard at the crocodile's belly. The crocodile rolled over in the water and then sprang back to attack him with equal fury.

<!-- source boundary: scan 95 → scan 96 -->

When Pavalayi's boat became caught in a crevice of a rock standing upright in midstream, Sankar left it there for a while, thinking it would be safe, and grappled with the crocodile in the water. The red blood pouring from him spread across part of the Kaveri. Within a second or two, the boat that had been safely caught at the rock was freed by the force of the wind and began striking against the waves and tossing about. Sankar left the crocodile and went to catch the boat. Weakened by Sankar's attack, the crocodile decided that this was its chance, leapt at him and prepared to seize him with its legs. It did seize him. Just as Sankar's body was about to be caught in the crocodile's jaws, he evaded them and fought the animal fiercely.

In the terrifying situation in which the fight might end within moments and Sankar might soon float as a corpse, Veeramalai Sambuvan sprang like lightning, snatched the spear from Maanthiyappan's hand, aimed at the crocodile and hurled it.

Struck by the spear, the crocodile lost the strength to fight Sankar and curled up. Sankar brought Pavalayi ashore.

Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu embraced Ponnar and Sankar and wept tears of joy. “Thambis! If you had not been there, I would have lost my treasures,” he said. His wife Silambayi and his son Vaiyam Perumaan revived Muthayi and Pavalayi from their faint and took them to a small nearby pavilion. Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu praised Ponnar and Sankar and also spoke highly of Veeramalai Sambuvan.

“Thambi! What would have happened if you had not killed the mountain snake and the crocodile?” he asked in amazement, patting Veeramalai Sambuvan.

<!-- source boundary: scan 96 → scan 97 -->

Maanthiyappan boiled with anger.

“Who are all these people you are praising? He killed the crocodile only by snatching the spear from my hand! Would the crocodile have died without that spear? It is because of me that this little fellow survived!” Maanthiyappan said, twisting his moustache.

The whirlwind that had been roaring “virr” gradually died away. Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu looked at Ponnar, Sankar and Veeramalai Sambuvan and asked warmly, “Thambis! What are your names? Who are you?” The three looked at one another. Ponnar was frantic to have the wounds from the crocodile bite on Sankar's legs treated. Veeramalai Sambuvan was just as anxious.

“Do not worry at all. We have excellent physicians in our Aarichampatti-Maniyangurichi. We can go there and have him treated,” Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu said.

“Should we not complete the karagam festival as we vowed in our prayer? Otherwise we may incur the Amman's anger!” Silambayi, Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu's wife, said anxiously.

“You and Vaiyam Perumaan take Muthayi and Pavalayi, release the karagams while standing at the riverbank, worship the Amman and come back. I will take these young men to our Aarichampatti-Maniyangurichi mansion,” Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu said.

Ponnar set off supporting Sankar by the arm. Veeramalai Sambuvan went with them. Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu called his men and ordered them to bring the chariots quickly. The vehicles arrived and stopped. Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu put the three young men into one carriage. As he was about to get into another, he went over to Maanthiyappan and respectfully offered his greetings.

<!-- source boundary: scan 97 → scan 98 -->

Maanthiyappan, the very embodiment of arrogance, dismissed him and sent him on his way.

Muthayi and Pavalayi, setting out for the Chellandiyamman temple with their mother and brother, watched the carriage in which Ponnar and Sankar were travelling.

Maanthiyappan, intoxicated by the beauty of the women from behind as they returned to the Chellandiyamman temple, forgot himself and stood motionless for a moment.

Then he moved. That movement became the motion of footsteps following those beautiful peacocks. He followed them.

When Muthayi and Pavalayi arrived at the Chellandi temple in the chariot with their mother and brother, the elders and distinguished women gathered there blessed them affectionately and warded off the evil eye.

“It is enough, Amma, to release the karagam for the sake of the *saasthiram*. Do not go out into the river in a boat again. Stand on the riverbank directly opposite the Amman shrine and release the karagams,” even the temple priest advised.

They did exactly that. After the *deepaaraadhanai* to the Amman, *kungumam* was distributed to everyone. Muthayi and Pavalayi removed their Amman adornments.

Even without elaborate adornment they remained images of beauty, and Maanthiyappan stood hidden behind a pillar, admiring them.
