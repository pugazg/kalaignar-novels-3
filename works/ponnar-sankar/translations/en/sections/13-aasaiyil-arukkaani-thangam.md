11

Arukkaani Thangam in Longing

The chariot bound for Aarichampatti sped away from the Chellandiyamman temple festival pavilion. Vaiyam Perumaan sat on the front seat. In the next row sat Silambayi, with Muthayi and Pavalayi on either side of her. To reach the Maniyangurichi region called Aarichampatti, they had to travel along the rough road beside the Kaveri, pass through Kulithalai and then head southeast.

Though Thamarai Naachchiyar could not come close to see her elder brother's children off, she remained inside the festival pavilion, blessing them in her heart and watching the chariot go. Muthayi and Pavalayi, those epics of beauty who seemed like paintings brought to life, would not leave Thamarai Naachchiyar's eyes. Even after the chariot had gone far beyond the pavilion, the faces of those young parrots seemed to appear before her, scattering clustered smiles like smiles made of Korkai pearls.

The scene on the screen of Thamarai's mind seemed to polish the brightness of her face. Yet, like sudden lightning, a streak of pain tore through that radiance. The heated exchange that had taken place between her and her elder brother at Aarichampatti when she married Nelliyangodan floated up and gathered in the waves of her memory.

‘Anna! Before you, my own brother, I swear upon the mother who bore me—upon my father

<!-- source boundary: scan 109 → scan 110 -->

too. You said that family affection ended today. It will not! It can never end! Anna, you mocked me by saying there was no house or threshold where ceremonial gifts could be brought. You are going to see me and my husband living in a grand mansion! My dear brother who said the bonds of family had burned away—you will come and beg me to give your daughter in marriage to the lion-cub son I shall bear!’

As soon as Thamarai had thundered those words,

‘If you are going to bear one, then bear two lion cubs! I will bring both of them and tie them like dogs at the entrance of my house to stand guard,’ Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu had replied mockingly.

‘I will bear them, Anna, I will! And I shall live to enjoy the sight of you bearing two daughters and coming in search of those lions!’ Thamarai Naachchiyar had cried out. To her, all of it now seemed as though it were happening at that very moment.

In that feeling she stood as an image of sorrow. Just as she had vowed that day, she bore two lion cubs. But within two years of those lion cubs being born and growing, she and her beloved husband Kundrudaiyaan had no idea where they had gone. There was no deity they had not implored, no temple they had not worshipped at, no sacred waters in which they had not bathed.

From the moment she saw her elder brother Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu's two daughters, Thamarai Naachchi's longing grew stronger.

“Just as I said that day, Anna too has now had two daughters. But the sons I bore are not here for Anna to come and plead that they should marry those girls! Amma, Mother! For how many years have we prayed to you? Has your heart still not softened?” Thamarai wailed aloud as she stood worshipping Chellandiyamman.


<!-- source boundary: scan 110 → scan 111 -->

The words that had burst and overflowed from his wife's heart disturbed Kundrudaiyaan. Wiping away, without letting her see, the tears that had risen to his eyes, he placed a hand on her shoulder and spoke affectionately in a pleading tone. “What is this, Thamarai? Why will you not listen when I tell you? How many times must I say, do not ruin your health?”

“Naachchiyaramma! No harm will come to your good heart or to your husband's pure heart. Do not worry. Just as night and day take turns, bad and good also come by turns. That is life. Is it not only because there is night that day has its greatness? In the same way, good has greatness because bad exists. There is darkness in your life now. You will see—it will surely turn into daylight!” Thanakkodi Chettiar, who was standing nearby, comforted her in a firm voice.

Even then Kundrudaiyaan, revealing the innocence of his own heart, looked at the Chettiar and teased him. “Well now, do the people of Aadhisettip Paalayam know fortune-telling too?”

“I know nothing of that. Nor do I have any faith in it. One fortune-teller said I would have ten children to inherit my property. So far not even one child has been born. That is why what I just said is not a prophecy. It is only a general observation about life—that is all,” the Chettiar replied.

At that moment, the sound of a horse approaching at speed was heard near the temple, turning their conversation in another direction.

“Who is suddenly coming on horseback? Is someone coming from our Kudaiyur palace? Or Maanthiyappan had come from Nelli Valanaadu—are they bringing some urgent message for him?” Kundrudaiyaan asked himself, Thamarai Naachchiyar and Thanakkodi Chettiar alike, and

<!-- source boundary: scan 111 → scan 112 -->

as he felt the hoofbeats drawing nearer, he stepped forward twice and peered out from within the pavilion.

The hoofbeats gradually weakened and faded, signalling that the horse had stopped at the entrance to the festival pavilion.

Everyone in the pavilion watched to see who was dismounting, but Kundrudaiyaan alone looked at the man who got down with astonishment.

Wearing a pure white robe, with a white beard touched by black, grey hair, a naturally imposing appearance and a gaze overflowing with kindness directed toward the pavilion, Maayavar dismounted from the horse. Though struck for a moment by a shock like lightning, Kundrudaiyaan immediately composed himself and understood that it was Maayavar who had arrived. Looking eagerly at Thamarai Naachchiyar and Thanakkodi Chettiar, he said, “The minister of Thalaiyur Kaali is coming. Whether he holds ministerial office or not, he is a great man—a noble man.” Then Kundrudaiyaan began walking toward Maayavar.

They stopped facing each other at less than a step's distance, motionless as they looked at one another. The moment did not last long. Saying “Vanakkam,” Kundrudaiyaan bent his head and bowed before Maayavar. Grasping both his shoulders with his hands and lifting him upright, Maayavar asked affectionately, “Well, Nelliyangoda! Are you well?”

“You are the minister of Thalaiyur! Yet when you come to a temple festival, you have arrived without any of the distinctions due to that office?” Kundrudaiyaan asked. Maayavar first answered with a small smile, then continued speaking with a laugh.

“How do you think a minister should arrive? With elephant troops and cavalry in front—

<!-- source boundary: scan 112 → scan 113 -->

infantry drawn up behind—and the minister riding grandly in the middle? I am only a minister; you are the border guardian who rules over many hills! Yet it does not look as though you too have made a grand visit here.”

Kundrudaiyaan answered Maayavar with the humility natural to him.

“I and a few like me merely administer border-guarding territories. You are not like that! Are you not the minister of the small kingdom of Thalaiyur? And not merely a minister—a great intellect as well! I only asked because your surroundings, security arrangements and everything else ought to befit the dignity of your office.”

“You know me. I prefer a simple life. Whenever ministers like me go outside, can we order elephant forces to parade as though we were marching to war? And even now, contrary to what you think, I have not come because of the temple festival. I came mainly to see you.”

“Is that so? Me? Truly?” Kundrudaiyaan asked in mounting surprise.

“Yes. Why such astonishment? Should I not come to see you? You are a son of the great Kongu Vellalar community who heap up harvested grain, tether elephants to thresh it and measure the loads. You are a worthy man who gives the labouring farmers as much grain as they need as wages. You are one who came to raise the honour of the Varagunnaa Perungkudi Vellalar lineage. You are one who swam through even the fire of opposition with a gentle heart and reached the shore. I came to see you—and your wife Thamarai Naachchiyar too, this mother who stands like the matriarch of Tamil women. I went looking for you at the Kudaiyur mansion. You were not there. When I heard you had come here, I came without caring that the route was difficult, full of forest and rough ground.”

<!-- source boundary: scan 113 → scan 114 -->

As Maayavar praised Kundrudaiyaan, Thamarai Naachchiyar stood nearby with her whole being filled with sweet delight. When Maayavar spoke a few good words about her too, she instinctively raised her hands in worship toward him and moved respectfully to one side near a pillar of the pavilion.

“How many years has it been since I saw you!” Kundrudaiyaan said with a sigh, then introduced Thanakkodi Chettiar to Maayavar.

Maayavar's appearance—his beard, his clothing, his movements, the sweetness of his speech—had left Thanakkodi Chettiar spellbound.

“When everyone had abandoned us, it was this Chettiar who gave us even a place to stay. It was with his support and help that we developed our farming, and through that our family gained much prosperity. We can never forget him,” Kundrudaiyaan told Maayavar with emotion, praising Thanakkodi Chettiar.

The Chettiar, embarrassed as though he did not deserve such praise, said, “Why praise me needlessly? Here before us stands this great man, Maayavar. Did he not leave after becoming angry with the king of Thalaiyur on your behalf? If he gave up a comfortable office and wandered in hardship for more than twenty years, was that not for your sake? Who else has the great heart to argue against even the smallest thing that is not good? Compared with the greatness of Maayavar, who owns such good intent, my merit is not equal even to the dust at his feet!” His eyes filled with tears as he spoke.

“You are making him stand while you talk. Could you not at least sit in that pavilion and speak?” Thamarai Naachchiyar said, glancing sideways at her husband, with the hospitality of the Kongu country showing in her words.

<!-- source boundary: scan 114 → scan 115 -->

“Yes, that would be better. Come,” Kundrudaiyaan said, inviting Maayavar. Maayavar went with him to the pavilion. It was filled with painted scenes concerning Chellandiyamman and Periya Kaandiyamman. The whole story the boatman had told Ponnar and Sankar about the karagam festival had been painted there as pictures.

A section called Naagamalai on Mount Kailasa was marked out in one painting. The next showed a five-headed serpent performing severe penance while thinking of Siva. The serpent was doing penance asking for the boon of a child. In the next picture were Paramasivan and Parvathi. Another showed Parvathi agreeing to be born as a child to the five-headed serpent and setting out. One painting showed Paramasivan bidding Parvathi farewell. The next seemed to show the five-headed serpent giving birth to a child. The following painting showed that child grown into Periya Kaandiyamman. Then came a painting in which Periya Kaandi flicked away her sweat with a finger and seven maidens appeared from it. Another showed Periya Kaandiyamman coming to the Vaazhavanthi region. Next was a picture of Periya Kaandi deciding to perform worship before crossing the Kaveri by way of Thottiyam and sending the maidens to obtain a karagam. One painting showed a Mannudaiyaan lying to the maidens that he had no karagam. A large painting showed Periya Kaandi cursing all the karagams in the lying Mannudaiyaan's house so that snakes wriggled in them. Another showed Mannudaiyaan begging Periya Kaandi's forgiveness and giving her a clay karagam. One picture showed a boatman mocking the seven maidens. Another showed Periya Kaandi causing his boat to catch fire after seeing it. Then came a picture of Periya Kaandiyamman and Chellandiyamman making a wager and releasing the clay and gold karagams, followed by a picture of the two Ammans reconciling, and another in which Periya Kaandiyamman explained Chellandiyamman's greatness.

Maayavar gazed without taking his eyes off the pavilion, which stood like an ancient picture gallery. Then he sat in the seat Kundrudaiyaan indicated and made Kundrudaiyaan sit beside him. Thanakkodi Chettiar and Thamarai Naachchiyar stayed in the festival pavilion rather than entering the mandapam. Thamarai Naachchiyar placed some of the *maavilakku* flour offered to the Amman on a small silver plate and brought buttermilk in a cup. She looked around for someone to take them to Maayavar.

“Amma, give them here. I will take them,” the Chettiar said, extending his hand. Thamarai hesitated. Whatever the circumstances, could she really make the Chettiar do such an errand? While she hesitated, Maayavar called somewhat loudly from the mandapam, “Let Thanakkodi Chettiar remain there. Thamarai Naachchiyar alone may come here. Kundrudaiyaan and I are going to speak about a family matter.” Carrying the plate and cup, Thamarai Naachchiyar came into the mandapam. Saying that the *maavilakku* flour on the silver plate before him was very tasty, Maayavar finished all of it.

Thamarai sat behind her husband in the mandapam. Maayavar looked steadily at her with cool eyes. There was a sense of pride on his face. The words that fell from his lips were firm and precise, like hailstones.

“How did that Maanthiyappan ever dare to behave wrongly toward this virtuous woman?”

When Maayavar asked this in pain, old memories surged through Thamarai's heart.

When she and her husband had gone to Chellaatha Gounder to ask for the property that was rightfully theirs, Maanthiyappan had tried to behave toward her like a beast. Even now, remembering it frightened Thamarai.

<!-- source boundary: scan 116 → scan 117 -->

Kundrudaiyaan quietly asked Maayavar,

“Maanthiyappan must have become a good man by now, has he not? He was young then. Now he has more responsibility. Though he is a prince, he himself looks after the whole administration. So he must have reformed, surely?”

Maayavar answered with a laugh. “Does everyone reform merely because responsibility comes to them? If you tie anklets to a chicken's legs, will it dance? Will it not still scratch at the rubbish? Maanthiyappan is the same!”

‘What intelligence and integrity Minister Maayavar has,’ Thamarai Naachchiyar thought, sighing deeply.

Kundrudaiyaan had been worrying inwardly over how to begin asking, ‘Why has Maayavar come?’ He steeled his mind and spoke.

“Forgive me. One should not ask a guest why he has come. Even so, I wonder whether I am wasting time without attending to the purpose that brought you here. Is there some matter you need from me?”

When Kundrudaiyaan asked this, Maayavar looked him up and down.

“Would I leave without telling you why I came?” he said with a smile. He tipped the cup in his hand to his mouth, drank the buttermilk and quenched his thirst.

Just then Thanakkodi Chettiar's voice cried out, “Ayyo! Come quickly! Come quickly!” Kundrudaiyaan and Thamarai rushed out of the mandapam.

It was the dim hour of evening, so figures were not clearly visible. Some people were running toward the Kaveri. Thamarai and Kundrudaiyaan looked at the Chettiar

<!-- source boundary: scan 117 → scan 118 -->

and asked, “What? What?” “Arukkaani Thangam has fallen into the river!” the Chettiar shouted, running again toward the river. By then Maayavar had rushed up. “What happened? Who fell into the river?” he asked in agitation.

“Our daughter, Arukkaani Thangam!” Thamarai and Kundrudaiyaan cried in one voice as they ran toward the river. Without delaying even a moment, Maayavar ran and jumped into the river. At that instant a horse shot away from that area with a “virr,” tearing through the air.
