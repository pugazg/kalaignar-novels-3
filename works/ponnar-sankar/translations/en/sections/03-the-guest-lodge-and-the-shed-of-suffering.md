2

The Guest Lodge and the Shed of Suffering

Knowing nothing of what had happened, Chellaatha Gounder, Maanthiyappan who had come as the groom, and the relatives who had come with them ate happily in the guest mansion set aside for the groom's party and then fell asleep. A few who could not sleep began playing *sokkattan*. Maanthiyappan's friends gathered around him, joking and teasing him about the wedding between him and Thamarai Naachchiyar that was to take place the next morning.

“Tonight you're with us; tomorrow night, will you even remember us?”

“Would I forget all my old friends for someone newly coming into my life?”

“You won't forget, Maanthiyappa! But the woman who is coming will make you forget. And she is Malaikkozhundha Gounder's daughter, a woman of magnificent beauty! When you are lying with your head in her lap, you won't even feel that there is such a thing as the world!”

“Are you telling me all this from your own experience?”

“You can take it that way!”

“It is true, as far as I am concerned, that I have come to marry Thamarai for her beauty and to find a new pleasure in life—but

<!-- source boundary: scan 26 → scan 27 -->

none of you knows that my father Chellaatha Gounder's brilliance in statecraft is also part of this.”

“Statecraft? What's that, man?”

“Do all of you know about my elder paternal uncle Kolaatha Gounder?”

“We have heard of him. We have heard that he carried out many reforms on the seventy *vallam* of land given by the Chola king, made it fertile and gave many families a livelihood; that he made Sirraalai Pattinam the chief town and named the region under his authority Nelli Valanaadu; that he created a new village called Valanaadu; that he made many lakes such as Vellaangulam; that all the people respectfully praised him by calling him ‘Maniyam’; and that Nelliyangodan was born to Kolaatha Gounder and his wife Pavalaathaal.”

When one of the friends briefly gave so many details about Maanthiyappan's elder paternal uncle, Maanthiyappan smiled and

“It was because Nelliyangodan was born that fortune struck both my father and me,” he said.

“What fortune, man? Tell us! Tell us! You're not going to sleep tonight anyway. At least tell us the story of how that fortune came!”

“They named him Nelliyangodan because he was a son born by the grace of Nelliyamman. The worry caused by Nelliyangodan's birth even shortened the lives of my elder uncle Kolaatha Gounder and my aunt Pavalaathaal.”

“What is this, man? If a child was born, why did they become worried instead of happy?”

“Haven't you heard that the nature of a crop can be seen in the sprout? Until Nelliyangodan was four or five years old,

<!-- source boundary: scan 27 → scan 28 -->

he did not speak at all. People in the village mocked him as a mute fellow, an unblown conch, and so on. Later he began to speak. Even after he began speaking, his parents' worry did not end! He did not know good from bad, knew neither guile nor scheming, and was a ‘Machchasaami’ who would take everything white to be milk. My uncle and aunt became embodiments of sorrow, wondering how someone so simple could protect all the responsibilities and rise to fulfil them. Both fell ill. They never rose again. On his deathbed my uncle called my father, entrusted Nelliyangodan to him, and asked him to stand by the boy, look after the administration of that region, hand over the responsibilities when Nelliyangodan grew up, and also marry him to Thamarai Naachchi, who was his customary match. My father promised and swore that he would do exactly that.”

“What happened to that promise now?”

“I told you about my father's statecraft, didn't I? That promise flew away in it. Nelliyangodan, who had been a Machchasaami and was confined in Sirraalai Pattinam in poor mental health, suddenly went off somewhere. My father searched and searched, but could not find him at all. If Thamarai Naachchi, who was meant to marry him, is married to me, the strength of the people of Maniyangurichi will also come to my father. My father can take over all the responsibility for ruling the territory that belongs to Nelliyangodan. My father is hurrying to conduct the wedding between Thamarai and me because if Thamarai were somehow married to Nelliyangodan, who has become a Machchasaami, it would affect the expansion of his authority!”

“Ah, man! Your father is a great Shakuni indeed! He has become master of someone else's property; he has made someone else's right to rule his own—and now, as though placing a seal on all his deeds, he is becoming related by marriage to Maniyangurichi and increasing his strength!”

<!-- source boundary: scan 28 → scan 29 -->

“In these times you have to scheme like Shakuni and betray like Vibhishana! If you hesitate, saying, ‘That would be unjust, that would be sinful,’ then office, prestige, property enough for ten generations, influence and authority will not even come within sight! If one person's prosperity and influence lie in another person's ignorance or suffering, then acting accordingly is what statecraft means.”

None of those friends came forward to reject Maanthiyappan's doctrine. The reason was that they were a group enjoying, simply by staying around him, the comforts of life that Maanthiyappan had obtained. Whether they liked Chellaatha Gounder's actions or Maanthiyappan's conduct or not, they were people who stayed close and savoured life on the principle that whatever gain came their way was profit. So they nodded, smiled, approved and cheered Maanthiyappan's theory of statecraft.

“What's all this noise and revelry? The wedding is in the morning! Only if you sleep well and wake up will your bodies be fresh! Go on, go to bed!” Chellaatha Gounder came shouting in a rough voice, and Maanthiyappan and his friends quietened down and curled up in their respective beds.

In Thamarai Naachchiyar's room, treatment was being carried out quickly to bring her out of her faint. Her mother Perumayi Ammal sat beside her daughter's bed with a grief-covered face. Events of the past moved like shadows through her mind. Perumayi was the one who had stayed beside her brother Kolaatha Gounder and his wife Pavalaathaal and cared for them when they died. She knew all the suffering the parents had endured when, after being childless for many years, the child finally born to them was lacking in liveliness and vigour and remained listless. Without causing harm to anyone—

<!-- source boundary: scan 29 → scan 30 -->

quietly accepting whatever hardships came, guileless, and having acquired the name of a boy who was “good for neither one thing nor the other,” Nelliyangodan had always stirred sympathy in Perumayi. Even now, the words Kolaatha Gounder had spoken to Chellaatha Gounder as he finally closed his eyes rang in Perumayi Ammal's ears: “The girl born in Malaikkozhundha Gounder's house in Maniyangurichi must be given in marriage to my son Nelliyangodan.”

But what could be done? Nelliyangodan had returned after wandering like a nomad, having lost his wealth and comforts, Nelli Valanaadu which had been under his authority, and its capital Sirraalai Pattinam. Malaikkozhundha Gounder had seized him too and, as a severe punishment, locked him in a shed swarming with *karaiyaan* and *sellu* insects. Even if Thamarai could not be married to him, it would be enough if he could only be freed from that shed and sent away. Not knowing what to do to make that happen, Perumayi Ammal kept sighing deeply.

As the treatment slowly began to work, Thamarai opened her eyes gently. Seeing that her daughter had recovered, Perumayi cried, “Thamarai!” and embraced her. Tears welled and streamed from both their eyes.

Locked inside the shed, Nelliyangodan writhed in exhaustion, unable to find even enough space to place one foot where there were no *karaiyaan* or *sellu* insects. It was as if all the flowers in a garden had been plucked and all the pollen gathered from them had been poured over a man from head to foot: *karaiyaan* and *sellu* spread all over Nelliyangodan's body. They crawled. They bit. To the eye it looked like an anointing with pollen, but under the attack of those wretched insects Nelliyangodan twisted and bent in torment. He could not stand. He could not sit. He could not lie down.
