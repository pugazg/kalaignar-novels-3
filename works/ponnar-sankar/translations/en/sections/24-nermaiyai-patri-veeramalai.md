21

Veeramalai on Integrity

After a few moments of silence, a voice rose in that hall of the mansion.

“I think our teacher Raakkiyannan has finished telling you everything that happened. So may I tell you what Raakkiyannan did after returning from Thalaiyur to Maarik Goundan Paalayam in deep thought, and how he acted to fulfil the promise he had given King Thalaiyur Kaali?”

It was Veeramalai who asked this. Everyone's attention turned toward him. Though there was anxiety in Maayavar's eyes, he looked at Veeramalai with compassion.

“Yes. The purpose of my coming is to relate everything I learned from Raakkiyannan. If you had direct involvement in what happened, you may tell it in your own words. I would prefer that too,” said Maayavar.

Veeramalai, who had been standing behind Ponnar and Sankar, moved them slightly aside, stepped forward, rubbed his forehead with his palm as though refreshing old memories, and began to speak.

“From Thalaiyur to Maarik Goundan Paalayam, it did not seem as though the horse Raakkiyannan rode had galloped at all. It seemed to have come placing one foot after another until it stood at the entrance to the camp. The boy Veeramalai was astonished to see Raakkiyannan looking so exhausted—

<!-- source boundary: scan 200 → scan 201 -->

—and sorrowful. He had never seen his teacher so weary. That heroic figure who usually walked tall with his chest thrust forward like a rutting elephant now looked like a tree that had swayed in a cyclone and come to rest. Veeramalai caught the horse and tied it in the stable, then stood bewildered, unable to understand the reason for the change in his teacher's face. Raakkiyannan entered the camp and one could only say he threw his body into the reclining cane chair; one could not say he calmly leaned back. The sound of the long sighs he repeatedly released spread through the camp. Veeramalai walked slowly toward him and asked, ‘Ayya, there is warm milk mixed with honey to drink. Shall I bring it?’ Raakkiyannan shook his head to say no and rose from the reclining chair. He kept walking to and fro as though measuring the camp with his steps. When his voice sounded firmly, ‘Veeramalai!’, the boy ran and stood beside him.

Raakkiyannan's hand stroked Veeramalai's shoulder. Again came the sorrowful call, ‘Veeramalai’—yet with his usual firmness.

“Has Azhagu Naachchi not come?” Raakkiyannan asked Veeramalai as he came outside the camp and stood with one hand pressed against a large branch of the guava tree there.

Azhagu Naachchi was Teacher Raakkiyannan's wife. Just as Raakkiyannan loved valour, he loved Azhagu Naachchiyar too. Their home was only within calling distance of the camp. Every evening Azhagu Naachchiyar would leave the house and walk to the camp. Raakkiyannan and Naachchiyar would stroll through the grove inside the camp, talking, laughing and spending the time cheerfully. Before evening gave way fully to the strengthening hand of darkness, Azhagu Naachchiyar would return home. Raakkiyannan would remain at the camp until it was time for the evening meal—

<!-- source boundary: scan 201 → scan 202 -->

—and only then go home.

“Amma came. She waited thinking you would come. It grew late and she went home only a little while ago. She told me to run and inform her as soon as you arrived. Shall I go tell her?”

Veeramalai replied humbly and waited to hear what order his teacher would give.

“You need not go. I will go home myself,” Raakkiyannan said. He pulled the rope of the large well's pulley, drew water himself in the heavy bucket, and washed his face, feet and hands. Veeramalai stood there watching every movement carefully.

“Veeramalai, you must do something,” said the teacher.

“What must I do, Ayya?”

Veeramalai asked with his usual humility. He could see that the sorrow which had risen on Raakkiyannan's face like a bank of white cloud was turning into a mass of black clouds. Clearing his throat, Raakkiyannan sat on the black stone near the well. He pulled Veeramalai close, gently stroked his head and spoke.

“It is nothing new. It is the same kind of task—going to Sankaranmalai. Go there, find out how Kundrudaiyaan is, how Thamarai Naachchiyar is, and how the twin children born to them are, and return by tomorrow night with all the details. If anyone there asks, you must not say that I sent you. Behave in such a way that they do not suspect you, learn the details, and return.”

“All right,” Veeramalai said firmly. Raakkiyannan replied, “I am tired—from the journey. I am going home to rest. Set out at once as I told you.”

<!-- source boundary: scan 202 → scan 203 -->

Saying this, he left the camp.

Following his teacher's order, Veeramalai set out for Sankaranmalai Fort. He was thoroughly confused. Raakkiyannan himself had taken him to Sankaranmalai Fort once or twice. Because his father Chozhan Thotti had worked at Aarichampatti, also called Maniyangurichi, and had earned Thamarai Naachchi's affection, Thamarai naturally felt fondness for Veeramalai, the son of Chozhan Thotti. So it was easy for Veeramalai to go to the Sankaranmalai mansion, learn what was happening there and return to tell Raakkiyannan.

Even so, why was Raakkiyannan so distressed about sending him there now? Why had the face of a man who was shaken by nothing become withered with worry? These questions dug at him, and unable to find answers, he walked toward Sankaranmalai.

Before sunrise he reached Sankaranmalai and found a surprise waiting for him. Hundreds of people were busily tying colourful festoons at the fort entrance. Veeramalai asked a warrior what the occasion was. The warrior told him that the naming ceremony for Kundrudaiyaan's children would be held the following night. Veeramalai did not wish to remain there any longer. Pretending that he was going inside the fort, he instead hurried back toward Maarik Goundan Paalayam. By two *naazhigai* after dusk he reached the camp and informed Raakkiyannan that a naming ceremony for Kundrudaiyaan's children was going to take place.

Raakkiyannan told Veeramalai that he too had received an invitation to the naming ceremony and ordered him to make preparations for the journey to Sankaranmalai.

<!-- source boundary: scan 203 → scan 204 -->

At that moment the sound of a horse's hooves racing toward the camp was heard. Soon a Thalaiyur warrior dismounted at the camp entrance, bowed respectfully to Raakkiyannan and said, “A letter sent by the King of Thalaiyur,” handing over a tube sealed with lac. Raakkiyannan opened it and read. He thought for a moment, then looked at the Thalaiyur warrior and said:

“You may go. Tell the king that I said I will personally complete the task entrusted to me. Tell him also that I will come in person soon.”

With Raakkiyannan's spoken reply, the Thalaiyur warrior mounted his horse and urged it forward. The horse flew away. The teacher looked at Veeramalai.

“Veeramalai, though you are young, you know well that I trust you. You must be the witness to something that is about to happen—a trustworthy witness.” Saying this, he handed Veeramalai the letter from Thalaiyur. “Read it and keep it safely. Azhagu Naachchiyar has asked me to come urgently, so I am going home. Everything must be ready in the morning for us to leave for Sankaran Malai Fort.” With that, Raakkiyannan left the camp.

After he left, Veeramalai read the letter by the light of the oil lamp there.

‘For Teacher Raakkiyannan's knowledge: I hear that Kundrudaiyaan's children are to have a naming ceremony. Use that festive event for our work and abduct the children. If you cannot do it, I am planning to send Thalaiyur warriors themselves. — Kaali, King of Thalaiyur.’

Those letters flashed like streaks of lightning across Veeramalai's eyes. Then another turmoil, impossible for his heart to contain, rose within him.

<!-- source boundary: scan 204 → scan 205 -->

It was Raakkiyannan's reply to the Thalaiyur warrior that caused it: ‘Tell the king I said I will personally complete the task entrusted to me.’ Did that not mean the teacher himself intended to abduct Kundrudaiyaan's children and hand them over to Thalaiyur Kaali? Veeramalai understood nothing. Why had the teacher entrusted the King of Thalaiyur's *olai* to him and thereby revealed the secret of this terrifying conspiracy? Was the teacher perhaps planning to abduct the children with his help?

Was the same man who had saved those children from the midwives' conspiracy now going to abduct them himself? Unable to reach any firm conclusion, the young man lay tossing and turning in the camp. Because the trainees had gone to their villages for the Pongal festival, he was alone and could not sleep. Even if the trainees had been there, was this something he could have discussed and shared with them?

‘Our teacher's heart is a good heart. Therefore, whatever happens will happen for good.’ Reassuring himself with this thought, he rose before dawn and prepared the horse Raakkiyannan would ride. A bag containing the things needed for the journey was placed beside the horse as well.

Raakkiyannan came ready to leave and looked at Veeramalai. “You too are coming with me on a horse.” Veeramalai hesitated briefly, then prepared another horse for himself, mounted it, and the two set out. On the way, Raakkiyannan told him what feat Veeramalai had to carry out at Sankaranmalai Fort. He also explained the reason for the course of action he had undertaken—

<!-- source boundary: scan 205 → scan 206 -->

—and Veeramalai's confusion disappeared.

That night Sankaranmalai Fort, decorated with rows of oil lamps, looked like a Karthigai festival. Many of the minor landholding chiefs under Kundrudaiyaan's authority had come for the children's naming ceremony. Raakkiyannan blessed the children and, before leaving, whispered a secret message into Veeramalai's ear and went out of the fort.

When the bustle of the festival had ended and everyone, tired after eating, had gone to bed, Veeramalai sat beside the children's cradles, looking at them with affection. Thamarai Naachchiyar brought sweets and told Veeramalai to eat. Then Kundrudaiyaan came and admired the beauty of the children sleeping in their cradles. After that Thamarai Naachchiyar called Kundrudaiyaan to eat. “Look after the children safely,” they told Veeramalai, and the two went to their meal.

Veeramalai, who had been waiting for the moment, rose quietly, covered the two sleeping children with a cloth, lifted them and ran toward the fort wall. On the far side of the wall, Raakkiyannan stood exactly as planned. Veeramalai placed both children safely into his hands. After handing them over, Veeramalai let out a deep breath, steadied himself, returned to the cradles, and began pretending to sleep soundly.

The palace serving women came to attend to the children and screamed, “Ayyo! The children are missing!” At that great cry Thamarai Naachchiyar and Kundrudaiyaan rushed in, shocked. They shook the sleeping Veeramalai awake—

<!-- source boundary: scan 206 → scan 207 -->

—and he rubbed his eyes and looked around in bewilderment as though he knew nothing. Crying “You wretch!” Thamarai Naachchiyar fainted. Kundrudaiyaan screamed, “Chellandiyamma! Why are you testing us like this?” The palace warriors seized Veeramalai, dragged him, struck him and kicked him, threatening, “Tell us the truth! Where are the children?” He said he knew nothing, so they took him and threw him into prison. When Thamarai Naachchiyar regained consciousness and opened her eyes, she said, “Do not do anything to him! He is a good boy. Poor child—he fell asleep. What can he do about the sin committed by some villains? Let him go!” And Veeramalai was released.

Kundrudaiyaan and Thamarai Naachchi had understood well that Veeramalai himself was now narrating incidents connected to Veeramalai. But when they learned that it was he who had actually carried their children away and handed them to Raakkiyannan, they were stunned. Why Raakkiyannan himself had taken the lead in abducting the children remained an unsolved riddle to them.

The children had been abducted. The abductor was Raakkiyannan. Those children had grown up and now here we stood as Ponnar and Sankar. We stood here as students of Teacher Raakkiyannan—and before the very mother and father who had given birth to us. If so, when we were children, did Raakkiyannan not take us to King Thalaiyur Kaali and hand us over?

With this insoluble doubt, Ponnar and Sankar sat in that mansion hall staring at each other. Their eyes had become question marks. The hearts of Kundrudaiyaan and Thamarai had become exclamation marks.

“Only now does a history of sacrifice begin,” Veeramalai said, continuing his speech.
