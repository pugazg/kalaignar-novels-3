12

A Cat in Battle Array

Even in the lightning-swift instant in which Maayavar jumped into the river, he did not fail to see who had leapt onto a horse from the riverbank and ridden away. It was Maanthiyappan. Though evening was closing in, Maayavar could still recognise him. But he had no time to think about it; the thought passed away like the shadow of a Garuda flying across the daytime sky and skimming over the earth, and he began swimming in the river.

Close to the bank, the head of the young woman struggling in a whirlpool kept sinking beneath the water and appearing faintly above it. Realising that it was her daughter Arukkaani Thangam, Thamarai Naachchiyar wailed, “Thangam! Thangam!” Kundrudaiyaan tried to calm her even as he prepared to jump into the river. Fearing that her husband, unable to cope with the current, might himself become trapped, Thamarai, even amid that panic, ran to him with the steadiness born of love and stopped him from jumping in. The men and guards who had come with Kundrudaiyaan leapt one after another into the river and swam swiftly toward the place where Arukkaani Thangam was struggling.

Maayavar overtook them all, lifted her up and swam toward the bank. This man, past youth and even past middle age, still displayed such strength and courage

<!-- source boundary: scan 119 → scan 120 -->

that all the people gathered along the riverbank were amazed. First aid was given to Arukkaani Thangam in the mandapam opposite the Chellandiyamman temple. Realising that she had swallowed a great deal of water and fainted, Thamarai Naachchiyar laid her face down and pressed hard on her back and shoulder blades. After the water that had entered her to the point of choking her came out through her mouth, Arukkaani Thangam slowly opened her eyes. Thamarai Naachchiyar placed her in her lap and began sobbing, “Thangam! Thangam!” Arukkaani, with a hand like a flowering shoot, wiped the tears rolling down her mother's cheeks and said, “Do not cry, Amma! I am all right.”

“Do not crowd around. Everyone go away. Let fresh air come in,” Maayavar said, sending out those who had gathered inside the mandapam. Only he, Kundrudaiyaan and Thamarai remained beside Arukkaani.

“You do not even know how to swim, Amma—then why did you do such a thing? And does anyone go swimming into a river whirlpool? Ayyo, what a huge pool it was!” Kundrudaiyaan asked his daughter with emotion.

“So the name Masachchaami really does fit, see! Did she jump into the river to swim?” Maayavar asked himself, smiling inwardly at Kundrudaiyaan's innocent nature.

“Arukkaani! What happened, Amma? Who chased you?”

When Maayavar asked her this with great tenderness, she gave no answer and shed tears. Water flowed from her eyes, but sparks of fire seemed to fly with it. Maayavar did not fail to notice. Concealing what he had seen as though she had not noticed him noticing it, he urged her, “Tell me without crying,

<!-- source boundary: scan 120 → scan 121 -->

Amma! I am asking only because I saw with my own eyes the wicked man who chased you mount his horse and ride away at speed. Tell me what happened!” The incident that had taken place a short while earlier played like a shadow before Arukkaani's inner eyes.

Arukkaani Thangam had come to the Amman festival with her parents. She had been walking happily around the temple precinct, looking at the stalls set up for the festival. Captivated by the statues carved into the walls in several parts of the precinct and placed separately on beautiful pedestals, she wandered in delight, admiring the sculptural work. When she saw dancing statues of Siva and Parvathi, she happily imitated their gestures and movements. Looking closely at each statue in turn, she came near the pillars of the front mandapam and tapped with her hand a stone bell wonderfully fashioned on one pillar. The stone bell gave the sound of a bronze bell. The moment she heard it, her excitement became boundless. Her hands, like lotus petals formed with red *kaanthal* blossoms for fingers, tapped the stone bell again and again. The pleasure of its sound thrilled the young woman.

At that time Maanthiyappan, who had been watching Muthayi and Pavalayi from behind a nearby pillar, saw Arukkaani. Remaining concealed behind the pillar, he feasted on her beauty with his eyes.

In the temple precinct and in the mandapams there were of course many statues of beautiful women with youthful bodies. He stood trembling as he realised that she seemed to possess, gathered together in one form, the finest beauty from each of those statues. He had already been circling around Muthayi and Pavalayi, but because they remained with their parents he had grown somewhat frustrated, perhaps thinking he could deal with those parrots later

<!-- source boundary: scan 121 → scan 122 -->

and, whatever the thought, he waited, moistening his dry throat with saliva, determined not to let the white dove that had come alone within reach of his hand escape.

After tapping the stone bell, Arukkaani placed her ear against the stone pillar in which it was set and, hearing a sweet musical resonance arise, lost herself in delight. She did not wish to leave that sound, which pleased the ear and gave vigour to the mind. She rested comfortably with her ear against the pillar. Seen from a short distance, she looked as though a beautiful statue had been carved onto that great pillar itself.

If the sight of that colourful peahen, youthful fullness flourishing from crown to sole as she leaned against the pillar in all her beauty, might stir even an ascetic who had renounced everything, how could Maanthiyappan—whose nature was such that he would stare with drooling desire even at a sari wrapped around a grinding stone—escape the fire of lust?

Without moving away from the stone-bell pillar, Arukkaani Thangam noticed the pillar behind which Maanthiyappan was hidden. But she did not see Maanthiyappan himself. Seeing a human sculpture on that pillar too, she wanted to examine its artistry and find out whether the tone of its stone bell was different, and she bounded toward that pillar.

In the speed of her movement, she did not notice Maanthiyappan standing close beside her.

“Slowly! Slowly! Watch yourself! Watch yourself!” Startled by a man's voice, she turned toward him and demanded harshly, raising her voice, “Who are you?”

“You have come to the place where I am standing and yet you ask me the very question I should ask you! Never mind. When a maiden asks, it would not be *dharma* to leave her

<!-- source boundary: scan 122 → scan 123 -->

without an answer. I am Maanthiyappan! Prince Maanthiyappan, the dearly begotten son of Chellaatha Gounder, guardian-ruler of Nelli Valanaadu and patron of Thalaiyur Kaali!” he replied with ostentatious pride and moved closer as though he had decided to swallow her whole.

The names Nelli Valanaadu, Chellaatha Gounder and Maanthiyappan had been spoken many times not only by Arukkaani's parents but also by relatives and the people of Kudaiyur, and had already sunk deep into her mind as names to be hated. Unwilling to remain there even for a moment, she let her face reveal what was in her heart and turned sharply to leave. Her appearance, which had been that of a young deer, suddenly took on that of a tiger. Maanthiyappan sprang across her path, spread both arms like a wall and blocked her. “Why are you darting away like this? What am I going to do—bite and eat you?” he asked with a mocking laugh, the fire of frustrated desire burning within it. Arukkaani's limbs trembled with anger; her body swelled and subsided with her deep breathing, and the changes in her form only heated Maanthiyappan further.

“What are you, Amma? A woman who has come down from the celestial world to see the festival? Are you Urvasi? Rambai? Thilothamai? I think you are struggling because you do not know the way. Hold on to my shoulder and I will show you the way without trouble—not an ordinary road, but the road to a world of pleasure!” Saying this, he seized her hand with his rough one.

If a woman gains strength even while fighting to protect her very life, would she not resist with still greater strength to protect her chastity, which she valued above life?

Arukkaani twisted her hand sharply and freed it from his iron grip.

<!-- source boundary: scan 123 → scan 124 -->

Her forearm and fingers had turned blood-red, and all the green veins stood out.

“Are you even a human being? Chi!” Arukkaani shouted.

“Do I not look human to you?” Maanthiyappan asked, moving toward her again.

“No! You are not human at all! You are some beast that found the moment when a man skilled in moving from one body into another had left his human body and entered another creature, then picked up that abandoned human body and brought it here!” Arukkaani answered furiously and turned away.

“Speak respectfully! Do you know who I am?” Maanthiyappan barked.

“I already told you—a beast! Do I need to know anything more about you? Fool! Do you know who I am? I am Arukkaani Thangam, the daughter born to Kundrudaiyaan and Thamarai Naachchiyar, the banner of their lineage! At least after learning that, leave this place!”

“Ah—is that so? Now that you have told me, my desire only grows stronger! I could not have the mother Thamarai; let me at least have this daughter, this lily!”

“Chi! See how fitting it is that I called you a beast? You have proved that you are the axe-handle born to destroy the Kongu Vellalar lineage, renowned for its culture! Hm—move aside and let me pass!”

Arukkaani flung the words at him like sparks and tried to push past him and leave.

“No. I cannot let you go! My horse is standing right there. I am going to lift you onto it and take you away. Come!” he said brutally,

<!-- source boundary: scan 124 → scan 125 -->

and wrapped his long arms, like the legs of a sea crab, tightly around her.

She slapped him hard across the cheek, slipped free and ran. He would not leave her alone. Arukkaani ran at speed and hid among the thick bushes above the festival pavilion. Even then Maanthiyappan did not give up. He kept chasing her. She darted into and out of the cover of the bushes, and he ran before and behind her, forcing her into desperate flight.

As she ran, Arukkaani reached Maanthiyappan's horse and fell to the ground. She rose and looked around. From beside the horse, Maanthiyappan bent to sweep her into his arms and lift her. Realising that even a moment's mistake would ruin everything, Arukkaani rose and ran toward the riverbank to protect the treasure of her chastity, which she valued above her life.

A deer can run so fast that its feet seem not to touch the ground, but does not the *vengai* tiger have the power to cut across its path, seize its throat and tear it? In the same way, Maanthiyappan ran across Arukkaani's path along the riverbank, intending to catch and possess her. Seeing no other way to escape, Arukkaani jumped into the river.

Some people standing along the riverbank saw a woman run in terror and fall into the river, and they were shaken. To protect himself from blame, Maanthiyappan mounted his horse and fled.

Thamarai and Kundrudaiyaan were relieved on one hand that Arukkaani Thangam had escaped from a great danger and disaster. Yet their pain deepened at the thought that Chellaatha Gounder's family was still acting with single-minded intent to avenge itself on their family and ruin it, and they looked at one another.

<!-- source boundary: scan 125 → scan 126 -->

Then Maayavar patted Kundrudaiyaan and said, “Nelliyangoda! Do not give way to emotion. Trusting in the strength of having Thalaiyur Kaali on their side, Chellaatha Gounder and his son are committing every excess they please. I returned and accepted responsibility in Thalaiyur because I believe I can bring that dance to an end and make Thalaiyur Kaali understand what is good and what is bad.”

Tears streamed from Thamarai Naachchiyar's eyes. She remembered the danger that father and son had once created for her husband's life and for her chastity when she and her husband had gone to Chellaatha Gounder's mansion to ask for the return of the lands, fields and other property that were rightfully theirs, together with their right to rule. It now seemed as though Thamarai Naachchiyar's heart itself would burst. She spoke to Maayavar through her tears, yet Maayavar could sense sparks of fire flying within her heart.

“Ayya! Of the children born to us, she alone remains. If we lose her too, what will we do? How will we live?”

Maayavar looked at Arukkaani Thangam with overflowing affection. Thamarai's sorrow did not plunge him into distress. The reason was that he knew her calculation—that Arukkaani alone remained of the children born to her and Kundrudaiyaan—was wrong. At Maarik Goundan Paalayam, Raakkiyannan had told Maayavar that their sons Ponnar and Sankar were alive, and so Maayavar did not share the basis of Thamarai's grief.

But one thing was clear to him: Thamarai and Kundrudaiyaan knew only that they had lost Ponnar and Sankar in childhood and remained immersed in sorrow; they still did not know how they had come to lose those children

<!-- source boundary: scan 126 → scan 127 -->

or through whom that loss had occurred.

Arukkaani Thangam, who had first lain in her mother's lap and then sat leaning against her shoulder, slowly rose to her feet as her exhaustion eased and she regained some composure.

Looking at her weeping mother and at her father sitting with tear-filled eyes, she spoke with martial fervour.

“I have lost both my elder brothers. You have lost two sons. Appa! Appa! I alone remain! Why should I not receive in myself the strength and courage of the two elder brothers we have lost? Give me permission from now on to fight against Chellaatha Gounder's family, who roam with the sole aim of taking revenge on us, and against the rule of Thalaiyur that stands in support of them! To remain patient even after the atrocity that happened today would be cowardice. Even if our whole family is wiped out in the righteous war we are going to wage against injustice, let the history of the future add a new chapter for us, saying that we perished for a sacred cause. Give me permission!”

Maayavar stroked the head of Arukkaani, who had been speaking with anger surging through her, and said with a blooming smile, “Amma, Arukkaani! Do not be hasty. Only if it were certain that Ponnar and Sankar were no more would you need to put on the garb of war!”

“What are you saying? Are Ponnar and Sankar alive? Are the treasures I bore still living?” Thamarai Naachchiyar asked Maayavar, reaching the very peak of eagerness.
