14

Toward Raachchaandaar Malai...

Just as the warriors closing in around the chariot expected, Vaiyam Perumaan shook off the sisters who were trying to hold him back and sprang like a storm, whirling his sword. His enemies were many; he was alone. Those who came against him carried many kinds of weapons. Vaiyam Perumaan did not trust only the sword in his hand—he trusted his own firmness of heart as an even more powerful weapon. Some of the enemies who attacked him savagely fell with their heads shattered by his sword-strokes. One or two lost arms or legs and tumbled headlong from their horses.

In the darkness, where one person's face could not be clearly seen by another, Vaiyam Perumaan fought back without the slightest fear even when many surrounded and attacked him, because he wanted to protect his family's honour, which he valued above life itself. So he paid no attention to the wounds made by spears that struck him. Nor did he care about the pain caused by enemy sword-points that slipped past his blade and struck his ribs and chest.

Realising that they could not defeat that young warrior in open combat, the rough band decided that they could win only by using some trick, and acted accordingly.

Beside the rises and hollows of the bush-filled road where the chariot had been stopped stood a great rock. Close to that rock was a deep pit, hidden so completely by leaves and branches that it could not be seen. The enemies fighting Vaiyam Perumaan near the rock noticed it. Planning to use the opportunity, they slowly moved backward as though retreating while continuing the fight. Vaiyam Perumaan, charging at them again and again in fury and anger, failed in that speed to notice the pit before him and planted both feet in it.

The next instant he was trapped in the pit like a prisoner. He tried to leap up with his sword in hand, but could not. By then the rough warriors had snatched the sword away. Nor was that all: they gripped both his arms tightly and hauled him out of the pit.

“Ayyo! Do not kill him!” Silambayi cried as she ran toward them.

“Not only your son, Amma—none of you will come to harm at our hands!” came a commanding voice. Vaiyam Perumaan and Silambayi looked sharply in the direction of the voice. Pushing aside the warriors near him, a bald-headed man built like a mountain of flesh approached. There was blood on the sword in his hand. In the light of the torches fixed to both sides of the chariot, Muthayi and Pavalayi were so frightened by the sight of him that they turned their faces away.

That mountain of flesh introduced himself.

“Do you not know me? I am Thirumalai! Guardian of Raachchaandaar Thirumalai! One of King Kaali of Thalaiyur's

<!-- source boundary: scan 139 → scan 140 -->

commanders! Since Raachchaandaar Thirumalai is under my charge, the king of Thalaiyur has made it a habit to call me Thirumalai as well!”

Vaiyam Perumaan's expression itself showed that he knew all this about the man even without being told.

The mountain of flesh called Thirumalai continued.

“It is my duty to carry out King Kaali of Thalaiyur's command. On their way back to the Aarichampatti palace after the Chellandiyamman festival, I was ordered to arrest Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu Gounder's two beautiful daughters, together with everyone accompanying them, and confine them in the Thalaiyur mansion at Raachchaandaar Malai. I have come to carry out that order.”

The harshness in the huge man's speech had now lessened. What showed instead was only his extreme concern with carrying out the command given from above.

Even though he was held in the warriors' grasp, Vaiyam Perumaan showed not even the smallest sprout of fear. Looking straight at Thirumalai before him without lowering his raised head, he asked, “Why should we be arrested and taken to Raachchaandaar Malai? Has your Thalaiyur Kaali begun playing games with women too?”

Thirumalai answered Vaiyam Perumaan's question with a laugh.

“Thambi, he is not doing this for play. This is an effort to keep the promise he gave Chellaatha Gounder, patron of the Thalaiyur rule and border-guarding ruler of Nelli Valanaadu.”

Hearing Thirumalai's explanation, Vaiyam Perumaan grew furious. Grinding his teeth, he shouted loudly.

<!-- source boundary: scan 140 → scan 141 -->

“What promise? What a remarkable promise! If he gave Chellaatha Gounder his word, is courage supposed to mean blocking a road and attacking at night like thieves? If you were men of honour, should you not have announced that you were coming with an army and faced us in broad daylight?”

“Thambi, young blood is boiling very hot! If even one division of Thalaiyur Kaali's army marched out, your Aarichampatti palace would become a mound of dust. Our King Kaali has no wish to invade and bring your rule under Thalaiyur's domination. His whole intention is simply to remain loyal to Chellaatha Gounder, who is both his patron and his friend.”

“Let your king Thalaiyur Kaali be loyal to whomever he pleases. What necessity arose for him to cross our path?”

“Our king does not interfere in anything without necessity. About twenty years ago, Chellaatha Gounder was humiliated in your Aarichampatti in the very presence of King Kaali of Thalaiyur. What, Thambi—do you not understand? Your father Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu knows this very well. The marriage that was to take place between his younger sister Thamarai Naachchi and Chellaatha Gounder's son Maanthiyappan was stopped midway by your grandfather, Malaikkozhundhu Gounder, the Maniyangurichi landholder. Because of the humiliation he suffered that day, Maanthiyappan has not married to this very day. Your father refused to give his younger sister to Maanthiyappan; at least he might have refrained from objecting to giving his own daughters. Chellaatha Gounder sent proposals several times, and your household ignored them. It is in anger over that that, with the help of the Thalaiyur king, a plan has been made to arrest you and all arrangements have been made to take you to Raachchaandaar Malai.”

<!-- source boundary: scan 141 → scan 142 -->

“You are taking us to Raachchaandaar Malai to obtain consent to give a girl in marriage to Maanthiyappan?”

“Do not say ‘give a girl’ in the singular, Thambi. Say ‘give the girls’ in the plural. Both your sisters, Muthayi and Pavalayi, are to be married to Maanthiyappan. Nelli Valanaadu has some twenty years of grievance—a vow to take revenge. You people of the Aarichampatti palace can come forward yourselves and help complete that vow. If you refuse and say it cannot be done, then at Raachchaandaar Malai Maanthiyappan is going to marry both Muthayi and Pavalayi anyway.”

When Thirumalai said this to Vaiyam Perumaan in a firm, emphatic and definite manner, Silambayi, who until then had been listening with tears streaming down her face, suddenly recoiled as though she had stepped on fire. “That will never happen! You may perhaps see our girls at Raachchaandaar Malai, but we will never allow you to conduct a marriage as you please!” she shouted.

Thirumalai did not yield at Silambayi's cry. He laughed loudly. Even through that laughter, the ring of warriors who had come with him tightened further around the chariot, Vaiyam Perumaan and the women. Thirumalai stopped laughing and spoke to them finally.

“I pity you because you are women. But what has been written on your heads cannot be rewritten. When even that fate is written well, why refuse it? No one intends to force your consent from you immediately, right here. Without being stubborn, you will come with us to Raachchaandaar Malai. There is a beautiful mansion there belonging to the Thalaiyur state. You will be held there. Tomorrow word will be sent to Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu Gounder. If he agrees to the marriage,

<!-- source boundary: scan 142 → scan 143 -->

all the problems will be settled. If he refuses and causes trouble, Thalaiyur Kaali will help Nelli Valanaadu march against Aarichampatti. The Aarichampatti Gounder and you, his son, will become slaves. Muthayi and Pavalayi will become Maanthiyappan's wives!”

Before Thirumalai had even finished, Muthayi and Pavalayi jostled against one another and cried, with sparks flying from their eyes, “That will not happen! It will never happen!” Each drew a *kattari* into her hand and raised it toward her own chest. Had even the time needed to blink been allowed, the songs of two cuckoos would have fallen silent. Before that could happen, the mountain of flesh called Thirumalai swiftly stretched out his trunk-like arms and snatched away both *kattari* blades.

Their mother stood distraught. The two sisters trembled in anguish. Vaiyam Perumaan himself was held by the rough men and could not move. Then Thirumalai's command crashed like thunder.

“Come, warriors! Set out for Raachchaandaar Malai. The women may get into the chariot. Form ranks like a fence around it!”

Having roared this, Thirumalai looked at Vaiyam Perumaan. “Thambi, now that the warriors have bound you, you cannot do anything more. My order is that all of you are to be treated with great honour. But I have to bind you in chains and take you in the chariot. Bear with it until we reach Raachchaandaar Malai,” he said.

Even as he spoke, several warriors came close and respectfully formed a tight ring around Silambayi, Muthayi and Pavalayi. By then the women themselves climbed into the chariot and sat down. Their faces shone with the courage that those rough warriors could do nothing to them

<!-- source boundary: scan 143 → scan 144 -->

because they had already reached the resolve to give up their lives rather than surrender their honour. Iron restraints were locked around Vaiyam Perumaan's hands and feet. As though even that were not enough, he was bound with chains and placed in the chariot.

To travel from the Chellandiyamman temple to Aarichampatti by the Kulithalai route, one had to pass through Nangavaram and Panaiyur. Though the village called Raachchaandaar Thirumalai was only a few *kal* from Aarichampatti, Thirumalai, commander of Thalaiyur Kaali, did not want to pass through Aarichampatti on the way to Raachchaandaar Malai. Even though it would be a longer distance, he had planned to take another route from the Kulithalai road to Thogai Malai and from there bring them to Raachchaandaar Malai without going through Aarichampatti. If they passed through Aarichampatti, they would have to face Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu's military strength there. So Thirumalai ordered that the arrangement made in advance—to reach Raachchaandaar Malai without coming within their sight—be carried out. He assigned another man to drive the chariot. Everything happened as planned. The chariot and the warriors surrounding it set out along the Thogai Malai route, while Commander Thirumalai rode ahead on horseback at their head.

The wounded from both sides—mostly Aarichampatti warriors—lay by the roadside and near the rock, groaning and rolling about, some barely alive. Lifeless, motionless bodies too lay scattered there. One of the warriors rolling on that road in the darkness paid no attention to the blood pouring from his ribs or to a *kattari* buried deep in him. Slowly, painfully, he crawled toward a horse standing exhausted near the edge of the rock.

<!-- source boundary: scan 144 → scan 145 -->

He was an Aarichampatti warrior, and the horse was the one he had ridden. That was why the horse walked toward him. As though understanding that he could not even crawl any farther, it came and stood beside him. The warrior struggled and tried to mount it, but could not. The horse looked at him with great sympathy. Then it went to a slightly higher rock, stood beside it and nodded its head toward him. The Aarichampatti warrior crawling on the road dragged himself farther to that rock, crawled up it until he reached the horse's height, and from there clung onto the horse. Even then he could not sit upright. He lay face down across its back, gripping the front with both hands.

Knowing that he was secure on its back, the horse began to run slowly toward Aarichampatti.

When Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu heard that an Aarichampatti warrior, barely alive, was coming toward the palace on horseback, he was shaken. He had been sitting and talking with Ponnar, Sankar and Veeramalai Sambuvan. He rose quickly and hurried toward the entrance of the mansion.

Wanting to learn what sudden shock had befallen the man who had lovingly brought them home and fed them, and to share in his suffering, Ponnar, Sankar and Veeramalai Sambuvan followed him to the mansion gate.

Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu ran forward and supported the warrior, who was drenched in blood atop the horse, and lifted him down. The warrior could not have remained alive even for another second or two. Before that, he struggled to give Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu his message. He opened his mouth, but his tongue would not rise. His speech slurred. The light was fading from his eyes.
