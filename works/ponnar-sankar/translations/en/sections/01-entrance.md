# Entrance

My younger brother Murasoli Maran urged me to write another historical serial for _Kungumam_. I hesitated, thinking that amid a thousand duties I would have to set aside time for it. During a tour of the Madurai and Mugavai districts, the conversation turned to the serial. Mr. Thennarasu, who was travelling with me, suggested that I could write the story of Kundrudaiya Gounder as the serial. Twenty years earlier, when I lived in Singanallur in Coimbatore, I had heard many people utter the names Kundrudaiyaan, Annanmar Saami and Ponnar Sankar. The oral accounts I had then heard of an emotional and moving history connected with these three names now began to play across my mind.

I began to study deeply _Ponnazhagar Enum Kallazhagar Ammanai_; the _Varagunnaap Perungkudik Koottam Ponnar - Sankar Varalaaru Nool_ compiled by Mr. A. Palanisami; and _Annanmar Swami Kadhai_, compiled by poet Sakthikkanal on the basis of poet Pitchai's palm-leaf manuscript. I discussed them with Mr. Silamboli Chellappan, Director of the Tamil Development Movement. I asked my friends Thiagarasan (Chinna Kuthoosi) and _Kungumam_ Pavaichandran to go to the

<!-- source boundary: scan 8 → scan 9 -->

places where this historical story had taken place and return after gathering details. I myself wished to go in person. People would gather around me. The expected purpose would not be achieved. Mrs. Subbulakshmi Jagadeesan was of great help to my friends Pavaichandran and Thiagarasan, took them to those historically renowned places and gathered some more information for me. (Later, a week or two after I had begun writing, I went in person and saw all those places.) I also had the opportunity to hear a tape recording of the Annanmar historical story being sung as an _udukkadi_ song on Coimbatore radio.

Because many people have studied it; because much has been told through oral accounts; because it is rare for any part of Tamil Nadu's history to be available in complete form; because these events took place roughly three or four hundred years ago; and because, in the passage of time, those events have been presented in different ways, a confusion arises like a small stone falling into a clear stream. Even so, no differing thought has arisen about the stream's essential clarity.

In the historically based fictional serial about Ponnar Sankar that I am beginning to write, the effort will be like raising a building suited to its foundation; there will certainly be no contradiction in which foundation and building have no connection with one another.

Scholars who have studied this history have not denied that the books published about it, notes concerning palm-leaf manuscripts, copper-plate charters and the like themselves give accounts that differ from one another.

One account says that the ancestors belonging to this heroic history came from the Thiruvalur (Thiruvarur) region of Chola country and created the Kongu country.

<!-- source boundary: scan 9 → scan 10 -->

As evidence for this, the following lines appear in the Kaliamman swing song near Kondisettip Patti, the clan deity of the Perungkudi group at Vathurampatti in Namakkal taluk:

“In Chola country encircled by the river Kaveri,  
leaving the venerable ancient town called Thiruvalur,  
together with the Chera-Chola called Thangam,  
to come and arise in the Chera mandalam,  
Kaliamman, sister of the majestic Madukkarai Chelli,  
the clan deity of the Perungkudi people of the Ganga clan,  
blesses the Vellalar, people of the clan that performed penance—  
the swinging dance of Pon Kaliamman called Paalai.”

Apart from this, another account says that they came from Thiruppathur in Chola country; another that they came from the Manamadurai region; yet another that they migrated from the Kolar region of Karnataka state; and another that they came from the Nellai country. These accounts reach us through research essays, palm-leaf manuscripts and old songs.

When one considers that the Kongu Vellalar community, described as an agricultural people and today spread across various districts such as Tiruchi, Salem, Coimbatore, Periyar and Dharmapuri, migrated from one part of Tamil Nadu to another within Tamil Nadu itself, and that the Tamil Nadu of that time covered a much larger extent than its present boundaries, it becomes apparent that they were one section of the Tamil people. It also becomes clear that, like a sailboat driven across the sea according to the direction of the wind, they had to change the places where they lived according to the circumstances that arose in those times.

The period when the rule of the three crowned Tamil kings—the Chera, Chola and Pandya—had extended across a vast and long stretch of land passed away, and they became, here and there, minor

<!-- source boundary: scan 10 → scan 11 -->

kings bearing their clan names of Chola, Pandya and Chera and holding narrow territories under their authority. It was in that period that the events belonging to this history took place. Some rulers bore the name Chola; likewise, some bore the names Pandya and Chera; they ruled fragmentary pieces of once-great countries that had broken apart.

That period too gradually faded, and a time arose when the British, Portuguese, Dutch and others began to encircle our land as a market for trade.

Before foreigners set foot here, people within the land had established tiny kingdoms and ruled them, and had then lost much of even those. It is in such a condition that this heroic history occurs like a shaft of light in one part of Tamil Nadu that lay in darkness.

Among the people of the Kongu country, who lived in various caste divisions such as Vellala Gounder, Vettuva Gounder, Mudaliyar, Chettiar, Vanniyar and Sambuvar, there is no evidence that hostility between one class and another is the seed of this heroic history. On the contrary, a _pangali_ feud that flared within one great Vellala family forms the central thread of this history. If it were a conflict that arose between Vellalars and Vettuvars, could members of both communities—then as well as now—have found a place on both sides in this history?

Not for one generation, not for two, but for as many as three generations, a _pangali_ feud that blazed within one great family takes many different forms and finally ends as a deeply moving historical epic.

Because the character called Kundrudaiyaan—free of guile and deceit, taking everything white to be milk—is the hero at the beginning of this history, there are also those who call it the “Kundrudaiyaan Story.” Kundrudaiyaan's elder

<!-- source boundary: scan 11 → scan 12 -->

son Ponnar gave rise to the _ammanai_ sung under the title _Ponnazhagar Enum Kallazhagar Ammanai_. And because Thangam, also called Arukkani, the younger sister of the elder Ponnar and the younger Sankar, lovingly called the two of them “Annanmar,” the _udukkadi_ song has also sounded as the “Annanmar Saami Story.”

The culture that runs through all these—the feeling of affection, firmness of mind, valour, love, the cool nature of hearts without guile or deceit, and the heat of the hearts of a few who make it their pastime to cause such hearts pain—will all be understood. With the desire to make the distinction of the characters shaped by them shine like a polished diamond, I am writing this serial story under the title “Ponnar - Sankar.”

Many writers of Tamil Nadu have created serial stories, books and novels with history as their background. Readers know well that I have written the historical fictional portrait _Romapuri Pandiyan_, taking as its background the history of the Chola and Pandya countries in the time of Karikala Peruvallathan and the account that an envoy from the Pandya country held responsibility in Rome during the time of Augustus Caesar; and the heroic epic _Then Pandi Singam_, drawing upon historical notes about the great warriors who flourished in the regions called Paganeri and Pattamangalam.

In Tamil Nadu, a land of many distinctions such as the greatness of Kongu, literary wealth, artistic richness, a spirit of valour and the dignity of guarding honour, one great evil called “disunity” has remained an incurable disease since the Sangam age.

My great desire is that such serial portrayals that I create with histories as their background should cure and end that incurable disease, and, at least in the future, give us the opportunity to see a Tamil land restored to health.

Ponnar and Sankar are revered as Annanmar Saami, and temples have been raised for them in the Kongu country. In those temples, the images of Periya Kaandiyamman and Chellandiyamman, described as their clan deities, are also objects of worship.

Thangam, also called Arukkani—the sister of the brothers Ponnar and Sankar, who possessed strength, venerable virtue and hearts of resolve, and herself a queen of chastity—is honoured as a deity not only by Kongu Vellala Gounders and Vettuva Gounders living in the various districts mentioned earlier, but also by people of many other communities. One can also see people of that region placing an image of Veerabagu Sambuvan, who was a close friend and faithful companion of the brothers, in some of the above-mentioned temples and venerating and worshipping him.

From those times onward, it has been customary to praise people who differ from ordinary human beings by bringing about extraordinary events through their abilities, comparing them with qualities attributed to divinity.

Even Valluvar did not disregard the qualities said to belong to _irai_, _iraivan_ or _deivam_; he desired that human beings should live as embodiments of those qualities.

He said that “Vaal-arivan” means one who can stand as the very form of pure knowledge, and pointed out that learning is of no use if one does not worship his sacred feet.

“Venduthal vendaamai ilaan”—that is, one without desire or aversion; he said that those who seek the shade of such a one will have no suffering.

“One who has no equal”

“Aravaazhi Anthanan”

<!-- source boundary: scan 13 → scan 14 -->

In all these ways, he set out the qualities of what is called God.

He believed that a person should live in this world possessing all this greatness said to belong to the divine.

That is why, through the Kural carrying the meaning, “So many rare virtues are spoken of as belonging to the divine. One who lives in the world standing in the path of virtue by which life ought to be lived will be esteemed on a par with the deity said to dwell in heaven,” he makes it clear:

“One who lives in this world as life ought to be lived  
will be placed among the gods who dwell in heaven.”

It is my assessment that Ponnar - Sankar, who are called “Annanmar Saami,” are people who came to be esteemed in that way.

Mrs. Brenda E. F. Beck, an Assistant Professor at the University of British Columbia in Canada, is a Canadian. She came and stayed in a village called Olappalayam near Kangeyam and published a research book about the Kongu people. The fact that she also studied the folk songs about the Annanmar story and published a research book makes clear the significance of the historical background of the Annanmar.

A map of the region ruled by Ponnar - Sankar appears in the books published by poet Sakthikkanal and Mr. A. Palanisami. I have included that map in this preface of mine as well.

Simply by looking at this map, one can clearly understand that the people of Kongu country expanded still further the area in which they lived from the regions they ruled in those days.

<!-- source boundary: scan 14 → scan 15 -->

Though we lie divided into so many castes that one can only wonder—and grieve—at their number, I too am one among those who look forward to the day when Bharathi's poetic words, singing of “one Tamil caste,” become not merely a dream but reality.

With that heartfelt feeling, and with the confidence that I will receive the support of all the people of the great Tamil community, I begin this historical serial story.
