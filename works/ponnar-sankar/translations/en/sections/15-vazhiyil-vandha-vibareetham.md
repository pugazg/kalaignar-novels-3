13

A Calamity on the Way

When Thamarai Naachchiyar looked at Maayavar and asked, “Are Ponnar and Sankar alive?” he said neither yes nor no. Instead, a smile curled at the edge of his lips. Even that smile had to hide between his long, thick beard and moustache before appearing like a lovely moon emerging through a torn black cloud.

“I intend to come with you to your mansion in Kudaiyur. I think it would be more fitting to speak about Ponnar and Sankar there than in this mandapam. May I come?” Maayavar asked Kundrudaiyaan.

“By all means, come. We can leave right now,” Kundrudaiyaan replied, overflowing with happiness.

How could Thamarai or Kundrudaiyaan, standing in the Chellandiyamman temple mandapam, know that Ponnar and Sankar were enjoying Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu's warm hospitality in Aarichampatti-Maniyangurichi? Even Maayavar, who had given them a drop of honey in the form of news about the young men, did not know that they were in the Maniyangurichi mansion.

Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu welcomed with deep gratitude Ponnar and Sankar, who had saved his daughters Muthayi and Pavalayi from great danger, and their close friend Veeramalai Sambuvan. He did not stop with welcoming them; he also took care to bring physicians to

<!-- source boundary: scan 128 → scan 129 -->

apply good herbal juices to Sankar's wound and heal it.

Because the fresh-leaf medicine could remove pain and burning and stop the bleeding at once, Sankar soon stood up well, looked at Ponnar with his usual proud bearing and bright eyes, and Ponnar embraced him. Seeing their brotherly affection, Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu remembered mythic figures familiar to him.

“Ayyo! You two have brotherly affection like Rama and Lakshmana!” he said, putting a finger to his face. Veeramalai Sambuvan immediately looked at him with a smile.

“Yes! And I am Hanuman to these Rama and Lakshmana!” he said. Everyone in the mansion hall burst out laughing.

“Only the tail is missing!” Sankar said with a mischievous smile.

“Stop the Ramayana right there! Do not go any further into exile in the forest, Ashoka grove, the burning of Lanka, the war section and all the rest,” Ponnar said. Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu did not fail to notice the composure and restraint in his words.

In the middle of their conversation the cook entered, bowed with great respect and said, “Master! The meal is ready!” Water was brought in silver vessels for them to wash their hands.

“Come then—let us eat!” Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu invited them with a pleasant face.

Thamarai Naachchiyar and her husband had once been driven out with the order that they must never again remain inside that house. Now that same Thamarai Naachchi's cherished sons, Ponnar—

<!-- source boundary: scan 129 → scan 130 -->

and Sankar, were being treated like royalty in that very house.

Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu did not know this truth. Ponnar and Sankar did not know it. Their parents did not know it either.

Sitting between Ponnar and Sankar, Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu himself took the dishes from the serving vessel and placed them on their leaves. “Eat plenty! The tuber fry in Maniyangurichi is very good. Even the *vadavam* served here has a taste of its own. The pepper broth is not too hot and is very good for digestion. Generally, you need not ask about thick curd in the Kongu country—and Maniyangurichi curd has a special distinction of its own!” he went on listing one thing after another.

“Listening to you is tastier than eating!” Sankar said. Ponnar greatly enjoyed his younger brother's teasing.

“Yes, and how long has it been since you arrived? You would not tell me when I asked on the Kaveri bank. You would not tell me while we travelled. Can you not at least tell me your names now?” Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu asked them, his face full of eagerness.

They had been eating with smiles on their faces, but now fell silent. “Oh! Is your mouth full of rice?” Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu asked as though making an innocent observation.

Everyone stared at Ponnar's and Sankar's mouths and almost forgot to eat, but the expected answer did not come. At last the meal ended. Everyone rose, washed their hands and sat on silk-cushioned chairs in the front section of the mansion.

Betel and areca were brought on silver trays. Ponnar and Sankar kept looking only at the trays of betel

<!-- source boundary: scan 130 → scan 131 -->

before them. Veeramalai Sambuvan came close, took the betel, folded it and offered it to them. Both hesitated to accept. “Do you not have the habit of chewing betel?” Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu asked.

“Even if they do not have the habit, they must take it. Only then can they keep their mouths occupied instead of answering your question!” Veeramalai Sambuvan said.

“There is no need to be afraid. I will not trouble you by asking your names anymore. There are comfortable rooms for you to rest in. They say even devotees become drowsy after eating—lie down for a while and recover from your fatigue,” Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu pleaded.

Ponnar and Sankar looked at each other, then at Veeramalai Sambuvan. It seemed to them as though Raakkiyannan were standing before them. They had unexpectedly come this far and had sent no word to their master. Deciding that it would be best to return at once, they looked at the lord of Maniyangurichi and said, “Please give us leave. We are departing.”

The Maniyangurichi Gounder, who had been sitting and talking, suddenly rose. “A fine thing you have done! My wife Silambayi, my son Vaiyam Perumaan and the daughters whom you saved must by now have crossed half the distance. What answer am I to give if they return and ask where you are? Do you know how eager they will be to see you and honour you with hospitality?” He delivered almost a small speech.

“Forgive us. Even coming this far away from our master and remaining here this long is something new in our camp life. What might he be thinking about us? How worried might he be? Where all might he be

<!-- source boundary: scan 131 → scan 132 -->

searching? That is why we must reach Maarik Goundan Paalayam quickly,” Ponnar said, rising with an arm around his younger brother.

“Do you think I do not know your master Raakkiyannan? I know very well what he was like in his youth, how rough his nature was, and how in those days his anger always sat right on the tip of his nose. I myself trained in warfare under him for some time. Back then he truly was like a volcano. Now he has become calm like a stream. Even if he scolds you for where you went, if you tell him it was to the mansion of Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu of Maniyangurichi, he will be very happy. Please listen to me. Once my family returns from the Chellandiyamman temple, see them and then I will not stop you from leaving immediately.” Giving some weight to the Maniyangurichi lord's overflowing words, Ponnar and Sankar looked at each other, wondering whether they should sit down again.

They were in a hurry to leave. Yet on the other hand there was an unnamed feeling that made them want to see those young women, Muthayi and Pavalayi, once more when they returned. Among those who knew this secret, Veeramalai Sambuvan alone understood it correctly.

Veeramalai Sambuvan had not failed to notice how those flower-creeper maidens had gazed without blinking at the incomparable young men who had faced the raging whirlwind, the coiling mountain snake and the crocodile to rescue them and bring them ashore. He had also seen and calculated how, on the faces of Ponnar and Sankar, which had until then housed only rough courage and manliness, affection, tenderness and a flood of feeling had settled thickly like frost over a rock. Therefore, although both of them were restless to leave

<!-- source boundary: scan 132 → scan 133 -->

Veeramalai could understand that those young hearts also carried a colourful longing that someone might insist a little more strongly and make them remain until those peahens arrived. So he came forward to speak for his friends, cleverly pretending that he was speaking in support of Chinnamalaik Kozhundhu Gounder.

“If we explain everything that happened to our master in detail, he will not be angry at all. I will speak to him in the proper way and calm him. Let us not disregard the Maniyangurichi lord's request. We can delay a little longer.” When Veeramalai said this, Ponnar and Sankar grumbled as though they disliked the idea, but inwardly they praised him—“This is what a true friend is!”—and offered him heartfelt blessings.

If bright-red vermilion powder falls on a white garment, no matter how hard one strikes and shakes the cloth, it will not leave it. The more one tries to wipe it away, the more firmly it clings. In just that way, it must be said, Muthayi's image had become imprinted in Ponnar's mind, and Pavalayi's image in Sankar's.

Whatever state these two were in, the same was true of Muthayi and Pavalayi, who were travelling by chariot toward Maniyangurichi.

Despite asking with great effort, they had been unable to learn the young men's names for the *archana*. With no other way, as Vaiyam Perumaan had jokingly suggested, the *archana* to the Amman had been completed under the names Periyaandaan and Chinnaandaan. As the priest recited the thousand names of the Amman and completed the *archana*, showed incense and lamps, brought *kungumam* on a plate and gave it to them, and plucked flowers from the garland adorning the Amman and distributed them, Silambayi's heart prayed, “May those young men be

<!-- source boundary: scan 133 → scan 134 -->

well, Mother! May they live long, Mother!” At the same time, the lips of Muthayi's and Pavalayi's hearts were saying only, “Will the Amman bless us to share our lives with those mighty young bulls?” It was the utterance of love, was it not? That was why it fell on no one's ears.

They had the same thought while travelling in the chariot. Many warriors rode on horseback before and behind the chariot for protection. Darkness had slowly begun to assert its rule, so the journey proceeded only slowly.

Silambayi was happy that both her daughters had had the opportunity to see Thamarai Naachchiyar and her husband Kundrudaiyaan. For some distance during the journey, she kept talking about it.

The dispute over Thamarai Naachchi's marriage had already happened before Silambayi came as a daughter-in-law to the Maniyangurichi mansion, so she knew little about that storm and had not been directly affected by it. She had heard that Thamarai had vowed that the children born to her would marry her brother's daughters. Silambayi had also heard that Thamarai had borne two sons and that, within a very short time, those children had disappeared. The dream that kinship might bloom again between Kundrudaiyaan's family and Maniyangurichi through those children had also vanished. Silambayi knew that Thamarai Naachchiyar had a daughter. If that girl were married to her own son Vaiyam Perumaan, there was a way for the old family bond to come alive again.

Would her husband agree to such an arrangement? Even if he agreed, would Thamarai Naachchiyar consent? Had not Naachchiyar's aim and vow been that the Maniyangurichi girls should be married to her sons? With that vow unfulfilled,

<!-- source boundary: scan 134 → scan 135 -->

why would Thamarai have any interest in fulfilling Silambayi's desire?

Silambayi sat in the chariot with all these different thoughts.

Ponnar's powerful build, his radiant face, the moustache that beat a drum announcing his courage, shoulders like an elephant's forehead split in two and set on either side—look closely and he was a lion, lift the head high and he was a *vengai*. Thinking of him again and again, Muthayi sweetened her heart.

What a heart, firm as steel! A moustache like two spear points placed above his lips! A physique that made one wonder whether it was a chariot platform or a chest! I have heard of heroic figures in legend—Arjuna, Abhimanyu, Indrajit. Have I not today been blessed to see one of them with my own eyes? Thinking this, Pavalayi sighed deeply and her heart swelled.

Amid the weaving of night, densely interlaced with black lines, their chariot moved slowly eastward along the southern bank of the Kaveri as far as Kulithalai, then from Kulithalai began moving somewhat faster toward the south. Having left the riverside road, the Aarichampatti route was broad and had few rises and hollows, so the horses could run without difficulty.

Even though the road was good, they had to pass dense stretches of heavy brush from time to time. Whenever thick bushes rose on both sides, the mounted guards travelling ahead of the chariot cried loudly, “Vel! Vel! Veera Vel!” At that great sound, jackal pups and wild hares hiding in the bushes rustled from one thicket to another. Some mongooses and hares that darted across the middle of the road fell victim to the horses' hooves.

<!-- source boundary: scan 135 → scan 136 -->

They had passed every brush-filled stretch. Only one place remained—a very dense, long tract overgrown with bushes. As the chariot passed through that place, cries of “Vel! Vel! Veera Vel!” rang out. But this was not merely the cry of the mounted guards who had gone ahead. It was the shout of many more horsemen who had surrounded them.

Along with those cries came the sounds of swords striking against one another. Mingled with them were death-cries—“Ah! Ayyo!”—that rose and then fell silent.

“Do not stop! Drive the chariot faster!” Vaiyam Perumaan shouted. At his command the chariot was driven rapidly. But it could not get through. A large number of mounted warriors stood in front and blocked its way.

The Maniyangurichi guards, caring nothing for their own lives, charged the warriors who had blocked and surrounded them. It was useless. They lost their lives and fell.

The clash of spears against shields and the scrape of swords against swords echoed throughout the area. Various birds that had flown all day seeking food and then settled into tree hollows, branches and bushes burst out in alarm at those terrifying sounds, beating their wings as they hurried elsewhere.

Some of the guards who had come for protection became corpses, unable to withstand the assault. Some rolled on the ground barely alive, their lives hanging by a thread. Some scattered in every direction, not knowing whether they would live or die.

The siege around the chariot tightened. Those inside did not know who the attackers were. Not one of the warriors who had curved out from the bushes, attacked and now surrounded them opened his mouth to speak. Vaiyam Perumaan drew his sword inside the chariot and prepared to leap down.

<!-- source boundary: scan 136 → scan 137 -->

With only one resolve—to protect his mother and sisters or die in the attempt—he roared like a lion cub. Silambayi used all her strength to restrain him and push him back into the chariot, but he broke past his mother and climbed down. “No, Anna! No!” his two younger sisters cried, gripping his hands tightly. Expecting that he might shake them off and charge, all the mounted warriors raised the spears and swords in their hands and closed the ring even more tightly around the chariot.
